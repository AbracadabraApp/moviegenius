A plane carrying James McCarthy, Bill Summers, and their Black servant Jeff crashes on a mysterious Caribbean island. They discover a mansion inhabited by Dr. Miklos Sangre, who's secretly conducting Nazi spy operations while experimenting with zombie-making voodoo. The film attempts to blend horror and comedy, primarily through Jeff's scared reactions, though these scenes rely heavily on racial stereotypes common to the era.
Mantan Moreland's performance as Jeff dominates the film, though his talented physical comedy is unfortunately wrapped in stereotypical characterization. The horror elements are minimal, with director Jean Yarbrough struggling to balance the comedy-horror tone. The film's B-movie production values reflect Monogram Pictures' notorious budget constraints, resulting in limited sets and basic special effects.
The film represents both the emergence of zombie films in American cinema and the problematic portrayal of Black characters in 1940s Hollywood. While White Zombie (1932) established serious zombie horror, this film attempted to create a horror-comedy hybrid, predating later successes like Abbott and Costello Meet Frankenstein (1948). However, its reliance on racial stereotypes makes it more valuable as a historical document than entertainment.
Early zombie films drew heavily from Haitian folklore and voodoo traditions, unlike modern zombie interpretations that emerged with Night of the Living Dead (1968). The film's World War II subplot reflects Hollywood's growing focus on Nazi antagonists, though the execution is simplistic compared to contemporary wartime thrillers like Foreign Correspondent (1940).
The film's primary relevance today is as a study of how early horror films incorporated supernatural elements from Caribbean culture, though often through a distorted, culturally insensitive lens. Its attempt to mix horror and comedy anticipates later successful genre hybrids like An American Werewolf in London (1981) and Evil Dead II (1987).
