Set in Mexico on the Day of the Dead in 1938, 'Under the Volcano' follows Geoffrey Firmin, a former British consul drowning in alcoholic despair as his ex-wife Yvonne returns in a last-ditch attempt to save him. Finney's portrayal of Firmin ranks among cinema's most powerful depictions of alcoholism, capturing both the character's intellectual brilliance and his spiraling self-destruction. The film weaves through one fateful day as Firmin staggers between cantinas, hallucinations, and moments of devastating clarity, while the looming volcanoes of Mexico mirror his internal turmoil.
Huston, in his twilight years, brings a master's touch to Malcolm Lowry's 'unfilmable' novel. Albert Finney's performance is a revelation, reminiscent of Ray Milland's work in The Lost Weekend (1945) but with added layers of colonial decay and political awareness. Jacqueline Bisset brings profound dignity to Yvonne, while Gabriel Figueroa's cinematography captures Mexico with the same haunting beauty he brought to Los Olvidados (1950).
The film resonates beyond personal tragedy to explore colonialism's decline, fascism's rise, and the weight of privilege. Like The Night of the Iguana (1964), another Mexico-set examination of Americans and Brits abroad, it peels back layers of Western entitlement. The Day of the Dead setting provides a rich metaphorical framework, connecting personal death with broader historical collapse.
Within the tradition of literary adaptations tackling addiction and decline, 'Under the Volcano' stands alongside Leaving Las Vegas (1995) and Barfly (1987). Huston's unflinching gaze recalls his earlier work The Treasure of the Sierra Madre (1948), examining how paradise becomes purgatory for Western interlopers.
The film's themes of personal and political apocalypse feel startlingly current. Its exploration of privilege, colonialism, and self-destruction echoes in contemporary works like There Will Be Blood (2007) and The Power of the Dog (2021). The consul's inability to face reality despite mounting dangers speaks to our own era's capacity for denial in the face of crisis.
