The Walker follows Carter Page III (Woody Harrelson), a gay socialite who serves as a companion to the wives of powerful Washington D.C. politicians. When one of his closest friends becomes embroiled in a murder investigation, Carter's carefully constructed world begins to unravel. Harrelson delivers a nuanced performance as Carter, a southern gentleman who maintains appearances while navigating the treacherous waters of political scandal. The character exists in a world of carefully maintained facades and unspoken arrangements, reminiscent of the social dynamics in The Remains of the Day (1993).
Paul Schrader's direction emphasizes the claustrophobic nature of Washington's elite social circles, with Harrelson's transformation into the sophisticated yet troubled Carter standing as the film's strongest element. Lauren Bacall and Kristin Scott Thomas provide excellent support, creating a convincing portrait of privileged women trapped in gilded cages. The film's measured pace and attention to social ritual recalls The Age of Innocence (1993), though without achieving the same emotional depth.
The film explores the intersection of sexuality, power, and privilege in Washington society, continuing themes Schrader examined in American Gigolo (1980). Carter's role as a 'walker' - a gay man who escorts wealthy women to social events - provides commentary on class, sexuality, and the performance of social roles in political circles. The film's treatment of gay characters shows evolution from earlier political dramas like Advise and Consent (1962), though it sometimes falls into familiar tropes.
As a political thriller, The Walker sits alongside films like State of Play (2009) and Michael Clayton (2007) in examining corruption within powerful institutions. However, it focuses more on social dynamics than conspiracy, making it more of a character study than a traditional thriller. The film's pacing and tone align more closely with European cinema, particularly films like Dangerous Liaisons (1988) in its examination of social power plays.
While the film's exploration of power dynamics in Washington remains relevant, its portrayal of political scandal feels tame by contemporary standards. Modern audiences accustomed to the complexities of shows like House of Cards (2013) might find the plotting overly restrained. However, its examination of how sexuality and social status intersect with political power continues to resonate.
