In this metaphysical ghost story, a scholar named Ho is tasked with transcribing a Buddhist sutra believed to hold immense power. Arriving at a remote mountain fortress, he becomes entangled with mysterious forces, including a seductive ghost and her equally enigmatic mother. The film weaves a complex narrative where reality, illusion, and the supernatural blur together, creating a mesmerizing exploration of Buddhist concepts of reality and desire. Ho's journey becomes both physical and spiritual as he navigates through layers of deception and enlightenment.
King Hu's direction reaches new heights of artistic expression, pushing beyond his earlier works like A Touch of Zen (1971). The film's languid pacing and three-hour runtime allow for an immersive experience where every frame is composed like a classical Chinese painting. Chun Shih delivers a nuanced performance as Ho, while Feng Hsu brings an otherworldly quality to her role as the ghost. The film's use of sound, from drumming to nature's ambiance, creates a haunting audiovisual tapestry that enhances its supernatural elements.
The film represents a crucial evolution in East Asian cinema, bridging traditional ghost stories with art house sensibilities. Like Kwaidan (1964), it elevates folk supernatural elements into high art. The film's exploration of Buddhist philosophy and Chinese mysticism offers deep cultural insights while maintaining its entertainment value. It influenced later works like A Chinese Ghost Story (1987) and Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon (2000) in their approach to combining spirituality with fantasy elements.
Within the wuxia genre, Legend of the Mountain stands apart by focusing more on supernatural elements and philosophical themes than martial arts action. It shares spiritual DNA with films like Dreams (1990) and Uncle Boonmee Who Can Recall His Past Lives (2010) in its meditative approach to the supernatural. The film's innovative use of landscape and atmosphere would influence later Asian horror cinema, particularly works like The Bride with White Hair (1993).
Modern audiences will find resonance in the film's themes of reality versus illusion, particularly relevant in our digital age of virtual realities and constructed narratives. Its visual poetry and supernatural elements continue to influence contemporary filmmakers, evident in works like The Assassin (2015) and Shadow (2018). The film's meditation on the nature of perception and truth remains startlingly relevant.
