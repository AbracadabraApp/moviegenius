Three delves into the complicated relationship between a married couple and a young woman who enters their lives, creating a tense emotional triangle that challenges conventional morality. Set against the backdrop of 1960s New York, the film follows Taylor and Bert, whose seemingly stable marriage faces upheaval when they become involved with Marsha, leading to unexpected consequences and emotional revelations. The narrative boldly explores themes of desire, jealousy, and sexual liberation at a time when such subject matter was rarely addressed so directly in American cinema.
Charlotte Rampling delivers a breakthrough performance that would later influence her roles in films like The Night Porter (1974) and Swimming Pool (2003). James Salter's direction brings a literary sensitivity to the material, drawing from his experience as a novelist to create intimate character studies. The film's naturalistic approach to dialogue and relationships prefigured the work of directors like John Cassavetes in Faces (1968) and Paul Mazursky in Bob & Carol & Ted & Alice (1969).
Released during a pivotal moment in American cinema, Three anticipated the sexual revolution and the breakdown of Production Code restrictions. Its frank treatment of adult relationships and moral ambiguity paved the way for films like Last Tango in Paris (1972) and Jules and Jim (1962). The film captures the shifting social mores of the 1960s, particularly regarding marriage, sexuality, and personal freedom.
As an early example of American independent cinema dealing with adult themes, Three bridges the gap between European art films like La Dolce Vita (1960) and the emerging New Hollywood movement. Its influence can be seen in later works like The Graduate (1967) and Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (1966), which similarly explored the complexities of adult relationships with unprecedented frankness.
The film's exploration of polyamory and non-traditional relationships remains relevant in contemporary discussions of modern relationship dynamics, anticipating current films like Vicky Cristina Barcelona (2008) and Professor Marston and the Wonder Women (2017). Its psychological complexity and emotional honesty continue to resonate with audiences grappling with questions of love, commitment, and personal freedom.
