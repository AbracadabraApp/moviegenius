Allan Felix, a neurotic film critic recently divorced, finds himself guided through his romantic troubles by the imaginary ghost of Humphrey Bogart. This clever premise sets up a delightful exploration of how movies shape our romantic ideals, as Allan attempts to navigate dating while constantly seeking advice from his cinematic hero. His best friends, married couple Dick and Linda Christie, try to set him up with various women, leading to a series of hilariously awkward encounters that showcase Allen's masterful physical comedy and wit.
Woody Allen delivers one of his most endearing early performances, perfecting the nervous, self-deprecating persona that would define his career. Diane Keaton shines as Linda, showing the charm and timing that would later make her an icon in Annie Hall (1977). The film brilliantly incorporates elements from Casablanca (1942), with Jerry Lacy's spot-on Bogart impression serving as both homage and comic device. Herbert Ross's direction carefully balances the fantasy sequences with realistic romantic comedy elements.
The film captures the early 1970s zeitgeist of sexual liberation and changing relationship dynamics, while exploring how classic Hollywood romance creates unrealistic expectations. It's both a love letter to and critique of golden age cinema, examining how we use movies as emotional crutches and romantic guidebooks. The film's treatment of masculinity and romance was ahead of its time, showing vulnerability in male characters when most films still celebrated macho stereotypes.
This film bridges the gap between classic romantic comedies like The Apartment (1960) and modern neurotic romances like When Harry Met Sally (1989). It helped establish the template for self-aware romantic comedies that would flourish in the following decades, influencing films like Manhattan (1979) and 500 Days of Summer (2009). The movie's meta-commentary on film culture predates similar explorations in works like The Purple Rose of Cairo (1985).
The film's exploration of how media shapes our romantic expectations remains startlingly relevant in our social media age. Its honest portrayal of post-divorce dating and male insecurity feels modern and relatable. The way it deconstructs romantic movie tropes while still believing in love speaks to current discussions about relationship authenticity versus idealization.
