Set during World War II, 'Cairo' follows American reporter Homer Smith (Robert Young) who becomes entangled in Nazi espionage while pursuing famous singer Marcia Warren (Jeanette MacDonald) in Egypt. The film attempts to blend musical numbers with wartime intrigue, as Marcia gets caught between romance and suspicion of being a spy. The plot weaves through Cairo's exotic locales, mixing comedy, songs, and spy thriller elements in an attempt to capitalize on the popularity of wartime narratives.
Jeanette MacDonald, known for her operatic voice and previous successes in musical films, seems somewhat miscast in this hybrid role. While her musical performances are technically accomplished, the transition between singing star and potential spy never quite gels. Robert Young provides adequate charm as the reporter, but the chemistry between the leads lacks the spark needed to sell the romantic subplot. Director W.S. Van Dyke, who had success with The Thin Man (1934), struggles to balance the disparate elements of musical comedy and wartime suspense.
The film represents Hollywood's early attempts to incorporate World War II themes into traditional entertainment formats. Released the same year as Casablanca (1942), it demonstrates the industry's growing focus on wartime narratives, though with varying degrees of success. The portrayal of Cairo and its people reflects the period's limited cultural understanding, falling into orientalist tropes common in 1940s Hollywood.
As a hybrid of musical comedy and spy thriller, 'Cairo' anticipates later attempts to blend genres like Silk Stockings (1957) and Funny Face (1957). However, where those films successfully integrated their elements, 'Cairo' struggles to find its footing. The film sits uncomfortably between the sophisticated spy narratives like The Lady Vanishes (1938) and the pure musical entertainments that dominated the era.
Today, 'Cairo' serves primarily as a historical curiosity, illustrating Hollywood's sometimes awkward attempts to address world events while maintaining entertainment value. Its handling of cultural representation and wartime themes provides insight into 1940s American perspectives, though modern viewers may find these elements dated and problematic.
