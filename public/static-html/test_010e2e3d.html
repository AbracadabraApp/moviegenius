The Crowd follows John Sims, an ordinary man who arrives in New York City with dreams of becoming somebody special. Through his eyes, we experience the crushing weight of metropolitan life, his romance with Mary, and their struggle to maintain dignity amid personal tragedy. The film's genius lies in making the everyday extraordinary, turning John's story into a universal tale of human aspiration and disappointment.
King Vidor's direction revolutionized cinema with its naturalistic approach. The famous skyscraper shot, climbing up a building to reveal rows of identical desks, became a template for depicting urban anonymity, later echoed in The Apartment (1960) and Brazil (1985). James Murray and Eleanor Boardman deliver remarkably modern performances, eschewing the melodramatic style common in silent films for something startlingly authentic.
Released just before the Great Depression, The Crowd prophetically captured the fragility of the American Dream. Its influence can be seen in Italian Neorealism works like Bicycle Thieves (1948) and later in films like Modern Times (1936) and Revolutionary Road (2008). The film's unflinching look at ordinary life was radical for Hollywood, which preferred escapist entertainment.
The Crowd helped establish social realism in American cinema, paving the way for films like The Grapes of Wrath (1940) and On the Waterfront (1954). Its innovative camera techniques and urban imagery influenced film noir and the city symphonies movement, seen in works like Manhattan (1979) and Lost in Translation (2003).
The film's themes of individual struggle against corporate anonymity and the pursuit of happiness in an indifferent world resonate powerfully today. Modern films like Nomadland (2020) and Sorry to Bother You (2018) echo its critique of capitalism and the American Dream, while its visual language continues to influence depictions of urban alienation.
