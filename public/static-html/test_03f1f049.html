Inspector Gadget attempts to bring the beloved 1980s animated series to live-action, following security guard John Brown who becomes a cyborg police inspector after a tragic accident. Matthew Broderick plays both the titular character and his evil robot double, but the charm of the cartoon gets lost in translation. The film rushes through its origin story and relies too heavily on slapstick humor that feels forced rather than organic to the character's inherent clumsiness.
Despite Broderick's natural likability, seen to much better effect in Ferris Bueller's Day Off (1986), he seems constrained by the mechanical nature of the role. Rupert Everett's Dr. Claw lacks the menacing presence of the animated version, while the special effects, cutting-edge for 1999, now appear dated and cartoonish in ways unintended by the filmmakers. The movie struggles to find the right tone between family-friendly comedy and action-adventure.
The film arrived during a wave of live-action adaptations of cartoon properties, following the path of The Flintstones (1994) and preceding Scooby-Doo (2002). It reflects late 90s anxieties about technology and human enhancement, themes better explored in films like RoboCop (1987), though sanitized here for family audiences. The movie also attempts to capitalize on the growing CGI revolution that was transforming family entertainment after Toy Story (1995).
As a family-oriented action comedy, Inspector Gadget falls short of contemporary successes like Spy Kids (2001) and earlier classics like Honey, I Shrunk the Kids (1989). Its attempt to blend practical effects with CGI places it in an awkward transition period between practical effect-driven films and the fully digital era that would follow with movies like Sky High (2005).
The film serves as a cautionary tale about the challenges of adapting beloved animated properties to live-action, a lesson that continues to resonate with recent attempts like Sonic the Hedgehog (2020). Its treatment of human-technology integration seems simplistic compared to modern explorations of these themes in family films like Big Hero 6 (2014).
