Lumumba chronicles the tragic true story of Patrice Lumumba, the first democratically elected Prime Minister of the Congo following its independence from Belgium. The film powerfully captures his meteoric rise from postal clerk and beer salesman to national leader, and his subsequent assassination just months after taking office. Through intimate scenes and political drama, we witness Lumumba's unwavering vision for a unified, independent Congo clash with colonial interests, internal divisions, and Cold War politics.
Eriq Ebouaney delivers a tour-de-force performance as Lumumba, capturing both his charismatic public persona and private struggles. Director Raoul Peck, himself Haitian-born and raised partly in the Congo, brings personal insight and historical precision to the story. The film's documentary-like approach, reminiscent of The Battle of Algiers (1966), creates an immediate, visceral sense of the period's tensions and complexities.
The film serves as a crucial examination of post-colonial Africa and the often violent transition from colonial rule to independence. Like Gandhi (1982) and Malcolm X (1992), it portrays a charismatic leader fighting against systemic oppression, while echoing themes found in The Last King of Scotland (2006) about foreign intervention in African politics. The film's unflinching look at neo-colonialism and international political manipulation remains deeply relevant today.
Within the historical biopic genre, Lumumba stands out for its refusal to simplify complex political realities. Unlike more conventional biographical films, it shares DNA with politically charged works like Z (1969) and Missing (1982), combining historical drama with political thriller elements. The film's approach influenced later African-focused political dramas like Sometimes in April (2005) and Shake Hands with the Devil (2007).
In an era of ongoing debates about neo-colonialism, resource exploitation, and global power dynamics, Lumumba's story resonates with particular force. The film's themes parallel contemporary discussions about foreign intervention, democracy, and self-determination, making it as relevant today as when it was released. Its examination of leadership under crisis echoes in modern films like Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom (2013) and Selma (2014).
