El follows Francisco, a wealthy and devoutly Catholic Mexican aristocrat who becomes obsessed with Gloria, a beautiful young woman he first spots at a church foot-washing ceremony. After pursuing and marrying her, Francisco's seemingly proper facade crumbles as he descends into paranoid jealousy and psychological torture. His obsessive behavior grows increasingly disturbing as he suspects his wife of infidelity with virtually every man she encounters, leading to a spiral of delusion and violence that destroys their marriage.
Arturo de Córdova delivers a tour-de-force performance as Francisco, masterfully portraying the character's descent from dignified aristocrat to unhinged paranoiac. Buñuel's direction is precise and unflinching, using surrealist touches to blur the line between reality and Francisco's delusional perspective. The film's visual style, captured by legendary cinematographer Gabriel Figueroa, creates a claustrophobic atmosphere that mirrors the protagonist's psychological imprisonment.
The film serves as a scathing critique of religious hypocrisy and bourgeois pretension in Mexican society. Buñuel examines how rigid Catholic morality and social conventions can mask deep psychological disturbance. Francisco's outward piety contrasts sharply with his inner demons, reflecting broader tensions between public propriety and private perversion that would become recurring themes in Buñuel's work, later explored in films like Viridiana (1961) and The Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie (1972).
El stands as a pioneering psychological thriller that influenced later explorations of obsession and paranoia like Vertigo (1958) and Repulsion (1965). Its unflinching examination of mental deterioration and sexual jealousy helped establish conventions of psychological horror that would be expanded in films like The Tenant (1976) and Possession (1981).
The film's exploration of toxic masculinity, controlling behavior, and domestic abuse remains disturbingly relevant. Its critique of how social institutions can enable and cover up psychological violence resonates with contemporary discussions about power dynamics in relationships. Modern films like Martha Marcy May Marlene (2011) and The Master (2012) echo its themes of psychological manipulation and institutional control.
