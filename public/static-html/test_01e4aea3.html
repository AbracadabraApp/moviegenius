Set in 1970s coastal Australia, 'Breath' follows two teenage boys, Pikelet and Loonie, who discover the thrilling world of surfing under the guidance of mysterious mentor Sando. The film delicately explores their journey from wide-eyed novices to risk-taking surfers, while navigating the complex emotional waters of youth, fear, and desire. Baker's adaptation of Tim Winton's novel captures the raw energy of adolescent friendship and the allure of dangerous pursuits with remarkable authenticity.
Simon Baker's directorial debut demonstrates exceptional control, allowing the story to unfold with the natural rhythm of ocean waves. Newcomers Samson Coulter and Ben Spence deliver unaffected, genuine performances that anchor the film's emotional core. Elizabeth Debicki brings subtle complexity to Eva, Sando's wife, whose presence introduces darker themes of adult desire and psychological trauma. The film's greatest strength lies in its ability to let moments breathe, much like the meditative act of surfing itself.
The film expertly captures the distinctive Australian coastal culture of the 1970s, reminiscent of the raw authenticity found in Puberty Blues (1981). It explores masculinity and mentorship without falling into toxic tropes, instead showing vulnerability as a form of strength. The themes of risk-taking and seeking meaning through dangerous pursuits echo similar coming-of-age narratives like Stand By Me (1986), but with its own distinctly Australian voice.
While surfing films often veer into either documentary territory like The Endless Summer (1966) or pure entertainment like Point Break (1991), 'Breath' charts a different course. It shares more DNA with contemplative coming-of-age films like 400 Blows (1959) and Y Tu Mamá También (2001), using the ocean as both setting and metaphor for life's deeper currents.
In an era of helicopter parenting and risk-averse childhood, 'Breath' offers a thoughtful meditation on the importance of facing fears and finding one's own path. Its exploration of mentorship, masculinity, and the search for identity resonates strongly with contemporary discussions about growing up in an increasingly complex world.
