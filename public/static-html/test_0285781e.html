A Couple presents an intimate portrait of Sophia Tolstaya, wife of Leo Tolstoy, through a series of monologues drawn from her diaries and letters. The film features a single actress, Nathalie Boutefeu, who embodies Sophia as she reflects on her complex marriage to one of literature's towering figures. Set against the backdrop of lush gardens and serene natural settings, Sophia reveals the joys, struggles, and profound contradictions of her 36-year marriage to Tolstoy.
At 92, Frederick Wiseman makes a striking departure from his documentary style, crafting an intensely focused chamber piece. Boutefeu's performance is a tour de force of emotional precision, delivering Sophia's words with both passion and restraint. The film's minimalist approach echoes works like My Dinner with Andre (1981), proving that compelling cinema can emerge from the power of words and presence.
The film resonates deeply with contemporary discussions about marriage, gender roles, and the often-invisible emotional labor of women. Like The Wife (2017) and Colette (2018), it explores the complex dynamics of a marriage where one partner's genius often overshadows the other's existence. Sophia's story becomes a lens through which we examine the eternal struggles between art, family, and personal fulfillment.
Within the landscape of biographical films, A Couple stands apart through its radical simplicity. Unlike traditional biopics such as Bright Star (2009) or The Hours (2002), Wiseman strips away conventional narrative structures to focus entirely on the interior life of his subject. This approach aligns more closely with experimental works like The Belle of Amherst (1976) and Woman of the Year (1942).
In an era of increasing attention to women's voices and experiences, A Couple feels remarkably timely. The film's exploration of marriage, creative partnership, and personal identity echoes contemporary works like Spencer (2021) and Portrait of a Lady on Fire (2019), while offering a unique historical perspective on these eternal themes.
