In the sun-scorched Arizona desert, Paul White works as a high-end audio installer while harboring dark secrets that slowly unravel as a series of brutal murders rocks the community. His wife Joan begins to suspect Paul's involvement, leading to a devastating psychological journey that tears their marriage apart. The film's narrative structure brilliantly weaves present-day events with flashbacks, creating a disorienting effect that mirrors the protagonist's fractured psyche.
Donald Cammell's direction creates a hypnotic atmosphere that recalls the psychedelic intensity of Performance (1970), which he co-directed with Nicolas Roeg. David Keith delivers a chilling performance as Paul, shifting seamlessly between charming family man and disturbed killer. Cathy Moriarty brings depth to Joan, echoing the noir complexity she showed in Raging Bull (1980). The film's unique visual style, with its extreme close-ups and kaleidoscopic imagery, creates a destabilizing viewing experience.
The film examines the dark underbelly of Reagan-era suburban life, similar to Blue Velvet (1986), but with a more experimental edge. It explores themes of masculine identity crisis and domestic violence, predating later psychological thrillers like The Silence of the Lambs (1991) in its examination of seemingly normal individuals harboring violent impulses.
While it shares DNA with contemporary slasher films, 'White of the Eye' elevates itself through art-house sensibilities reminiscent of Don't Look Now (1973). Its influence can be seen in later psychological thrillers like Lost Highway (1997) and Memento (2000), particularly in its non-linear storytelling and exploration of fractured identity.
The film's examination of domestic violence and suburban malaise remains painfully relevant, while its experimental visual techniques have influenced modern psychological thrillers like Under the Skin (2013) and You Were Never Really Here (2017). Its exploration of masculinity in crisis resonates with contemporary discussions about toxic masculinity and violence.
