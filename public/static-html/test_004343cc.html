The Locket unfolds through an innovative nested flashback structure, beginning on Nancy's wedding day when a mysterious man appears to warn her fiancé about her dark past. What follows is a psychological maze of memories and revelations, each layer peeling back to expose the truth about Nancy's kleptomaniac tendencies and possible involvement in a murder. The story's complexity rivals Citizen Kane (1941) in its narrative ambition, weaving together multiple perspectives that challenge viewers to piece together the truth about its enigmatic central character.
Laraine Day delivers a tour-de-force performance as Nancy, creating a character whose charm and vulnerability mask a deeply disturbed psyche. Robert Mitchum, early in his career, brings his characteristic noir presence to a supporting role. Director John Brahm, working with cinematographer Nicholas Musuraca, crafts a visual atmosphere that mirrors the psychological complexity of the story, using shadows and light in ways that recall the masterful technique of Double Indemnity (1944).
The film stands as a fascinating exploration of post-war anxiety about identity and truth. Like The Woman in the Window (1944) and Laura (1944), it delves into questions of perception and reality, reflecting society's growing interest in psychology and the hidden depths of human nature. Nancy's character represents a complex portrayal of female psychology rarely seen in 1940s cinema, predating similar character studies in films like Marnie (1964).
Within the film noir genre, The Locket stands out for its psychological complexity and narrative innovation. While other noirs of the era like The Big Sleep (1946) focused on detective stories, this film pushes the boundaries of the genre by emphasizing psychological drama over crime elements. Its influence can be seen in later psychological thrillers like Vertigo (1958) and modern neo-noirs.
The film's exploration of unreliable narration and psychological complexity feels remarkably modern, anticipating techniques used in films like Memento (2000) and Inception (2010). Its examination of truth, memory, and perception remains relevant to contemporary discussions about narrative reliability and psychological trauma in cinema.
