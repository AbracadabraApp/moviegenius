Orson Welles crafts a masterful distillation of Shakespeare's Henry IV plays, centering on the tragic figure of Sir John Falstaff and his complex relationship with Prince Hal. The film follows Falstaff, a boisterous knight and tavern regular, as he mentors the young prince in the ways of life beyond the court. Their friendship faces its ultimate test when Hal must choose between his surrogate father Falstaff and his royal duties as the future King Henry V. Welles inhabits Falstaff with tremendous pathos, creating a character who is simultaneously comedic and deeply tragic.
Welles delivers what many consider his finest performance, bringing both ribald humor and crushing vulnerability to Falstaff. John Gielgud's King Henry IV provides the perfect counterpoint, embodying regal authority and paternal disappointment. The film's technical achievements are extraordinary given its modest budget, with Welles using creative camera angles and innovative editing to create a sense of scope that rivals the grandest Hollywood productions. The Battle of Shrewsbury sequence remains a masterclass in filming medieval combat, influencing scenes in Braveheart (1995) and Game of Thrones (2011-2019).
The film resonates deeply with themes of father-son relationships, loyalty, and the burden of power. Like The Godfather (1972) would later explore, it examines how political necessity corrupts personal bonds. Welles' interpretation speaks to the death of medieval values in the face of modern political pragmatism, a theme that echoes through works like The Lion in Winter (1968) and The Last Duel (2021).
Within Shakespeare adaptations, Chimes at Midnight stands alongside Throne of Blood (1957) and Richard III (1955) as a film that transcends mere translation of text to screen. It reinvents the historical epic genre, focusing on intimate character dynamics rather than spectacle, influencing later works like The King (2019) and Macbeth (2015).
The film's exploration of mentorship, betrayal, and the conflict between personal loyalty and political duty remains startlingly relevant. Its influence can be seen in contemporary works examining power and friendship, from The Crown (2016-) to The Favourite (2018). The technical innovations in filming battle sequences continue to influence modern directors tackling historical subjects.
