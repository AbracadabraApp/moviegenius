Skateboard follows Manny Bloom, a down-on-his-luck manager who discovers a group of talented teenage skateboarders and forms them into a professional team to pay off his gambling debts. The story centers on the relationship between Manny and young skateboarding prodigy Brad Harris, as they navigate competitions and personal challenges. While the premise had potential, the execution lacks the depth and authenticity that later skateboarding films would achieve.
Allen Garfield brings a workmanlike charm to Manny, while teen idol Leif Garrett's presence speaks to the era's youth marketing. The film's greatest strength lies in its authentic skateboarding sequences, which capture the emerging sport's energy, though technical limitations of the time restrict their impact. Director George Gage approaches the material with enthusiasm but struggles to elevate it beyond basic sports movie tropes.
Released during skateboarding's first major boom in the late 1970s, the film represents Hollywood's early attempts to capitalize on youth counter-culture. While it predates the more nuanced exploration of skating culture seen in films like Thrashin' (1986) and Gleaming the Cube (1989), it serves as a time capsule of the sport's commercialization and growing mainstream appeal.
Skateboard sits at the intersection of sports drama and youth exploitation films of the 1970s, following similar beats to films like The Bad News Bears (1976). It helped establish tropes that would be refined in later skateboarding films, though its approach feels primitive compared to modern entries like Mid90s (2018) or Lords of Dogtown (2005).
While historically significant as one of the first major skateboarding films, its relevance today is primarily academic. Modern audiences accustomed to the technical sophistication of films like Street Dreams (2009) may find the action sequences quaint, though the film's documentation of early skating culture holds anthropological value.
