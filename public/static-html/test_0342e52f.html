This revealing documentary chronicles the life and career of Gene Tierney, one of classic Hollywood's most enigmatic stars. Through carefully curated archival footage and interviews, the film traces Tierney's journey from her breakthrough in Laura (1944) to her struggles with mental illness and eventual retreat from Hollywood. The documentary doesn't shy away from difficult topics, including Tierney's tragic experiences during pregnancy when a fan with rubella infected her, leading to her daughter being born disabled.
The film masterfully weaves together clips from Tierney's most notable performances, including her haunting turn in Leave Her to Heaven (1945) and her sophisticated portrayal in The Ghost and Mrs. Muir (1947). Through interviews with film historians and surviving contemporaries, we get a sense of Tierney's unique screen presence - a combination of ethereal beauty and raw emotional depth that set her apart from other actresses of the era.
This documentary serves as both a celebration of Tierney's talent and a critique of Hollywood's treatment of mental health issues during the studio era. It draws parallels to other stars who struggled, like Vivien Leigh, while highlighting how Tierney's openness about her battles with depression and mental illness was groundbreaking for its time. The film contextualizes her story within the broader narrative of women in Hollywood, particularly regarding aging and the pressure to maintain a perfect public image.
As a Hollywood documentary, this film stands alongside works like The Kid Stays in the Picture (2002) and Bright Lights (2016) in its intimate portrayal of star life. However, it distinguishes itself through its focus on mental health advocacy and its exploration of the often-overlooked aspects of Golden Age Hollywood.
The documentary resonates strongly with contemporary discussions about mental health in the entertainment industry and women's struggles in Hollywood. It draws clear lines between past and present, making Tierney's story feel remarkably relevant to modern audiences, especially in light of recent conversations about celebrity mental health sparked by documentaries like Framing Britney Spears (2021).
