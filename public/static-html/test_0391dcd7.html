This modern reimagining of Jane Austen's final completed novel follows Anne Elliot, played by Dakota Johnson, who breaks the fourth wall to narrate her story of lost love and second chances. Eight years after being persuaded to reject Frederick Wentworth, Anne encounters him again, now a successful naval captain. The film attempts to blend Austen's period setting with contemporary sensibilities, incorporating modern language and direct-to-camera addresses reminiscent of Fleabag (2016).
Dakota Johnson brings a contemporary flair to Anne Elliot, though her performance often feels more suited to a modern romantic comedy than Austen's nuanced character study. The direction by Carrie Cracknell, making her feature debut after success in theater, struggles to find the right balance between period authenticity and modern appeal. The supporting cast, including Henry Golding as Mr. Elliot, delivers serviceable but unmemorable performances that lack the sharp wit and social commentary present in better Austen adaptations.
While attempting to make Austen accessible to modern audiences, the film inadvertently strips away the author's careful examination of class, social obligations, and women's limited agency in Regency England. The modernized dialogue and fourth-wall breaks, while clearly inspired by successful productions like Bridgerton (2020), often undermine the story's historical context and emotional depth.
This adaptation follows a trend of modernizing classic literature, similar to Emma (2020) and Little Women (2019), but lacks their successful balance of contemporary relevance and period authenticity. The film's approach feels more aligned with recent teen adaptations like Fire Island (2022) or Clueless (1995), yet without their clever translation of Austen's themes to new settings.
While the film attempts to make Austen relevant to modern viewers through contemporary language and direct address, it misses opportunities to meaningfully explore themes of regret, second chances, and social pressure that remain deeply relevant today. The modernization feels superficial rather than insightful, unlike successful updates such as Bridget Jones's Diary (2001).
