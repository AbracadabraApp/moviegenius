Diana attempts to chronicle the last two years of Princess Diana's life, focusing on her relationship with heart surgeon Hasnat Khan and her transformation into a global humanitarian figure. The film portrays her navigation of media attention while pursuing a private romance, culminating in her tragic death in 1997. Naomi Watts takes on the challenging role of the People's Princess, attempting to capture both her public persona and private struggles.
Despite Watts' earnest efforts, the performance never fully transcends mere imitation. The film struggles to find depth beneath the surface-level recreation of iconic moments and famous interviews. Director Oliver Hirschbiegel, who brilliantly depicted Hitler's final days in Downfall (2004), fails to bring the same psychological complexity to Diana's story. The result feels more like a glossy television movie than a probing character study.
The film raises important questions about celebrity culture, media intrusion, and the human cost of public life, but addresses them with disappointing superficiality. Unlike The Queen (2006) or Spencer (2021), which offered nuanced examinations of royal life, Diana opts for melodrama over meaningful insight. The treatment of the paparazzi's role in Diana's life feels particularly simplified compared to documentaries like The Story of Diana (2017).
Within the realm of royal biopics, Diana stands as a cautionary tale of how not to approach such sensitive material. Where films like The King's Speech (2010) and The Young Victoria (2009) found humanity in their royal subjects, this film reduces a complex woman to romantic clichés. The contrast is particularly stark when compared to Pablo Larraín's Jackie (2016), which showed how to imaginatively explore a famous woman's private grief.
The film's shortcomings feel even more pronounced in light of recent discussions about media treatment of royal figures, as seen in coverage of Meghan Markle. Modern viewers might find more relevant insights in documentaries like Diana: In Her Own Words (2017) or the analytical depth of The Crown (2016-present).
