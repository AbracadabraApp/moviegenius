Set in 1986 Brooklyn, this semi-autobiographical tale follows the Berkman family's dissolution through divorce. Bernard (Jeff Daniels), a once-promising novelist now teaching creative writing, and Joan (Laura Linney), an emerging literary success, split their household and children between them. Their sons Walt (Jesse Eisenberg) and Frank (Owen Kline) become pawns in an intellectual cold war, taking sides and mimicking their parents' pretensions and flaws. Walt parrots his father's pompous literary opinions without understanding them, while younger Frank acts out in increasingly concerning ways.
Baumbach directs with remarkable restraint, allowing his ensemble to bring raw authenticity to every scene. Jeff Daniels delivers a career-best performance as Bernard, a man whose intellectual armor can't hide his deep insecurities. Laura Linney perfectly captures Joan's complex mix of maternal guilt and personal liberation. The film's handheld 16mm photography creates an intimate, almost documentary-like atmosphere that enhances the emotional authenticity of every interaction.
The film masterfully explores how intellectual pretension can mask emotional immaturity, particularly in academic circles. It captures a specific moment in Brooklyn's cultural evolution, when literary ambition and academic status were currency among a certain social set. Like Marriage Story (2019), it shows how divorce can turn children into unwitting participants in their parents' warfare, but with a uniquely sardonic edge reminiscent of The Royal Tenenbaums (2001).
Within the indie family drama landscape, The Squid and the Whale stands out for its unflinching honesty and dark humor. It shares DNA with films like The Ice Storm (1997) and Wonder Boys (2000) in its exploration of intellectual family dysfunction, while paving the way for later works like Lady Bird (2017) and The Meyerowitz Stories (2017) in its blend of comedy and familial pain.
The film's examination of how children internalize their parents' failures remains painfully relevant. Its exploration of masculine insecurity, intellectual posturing, and the impact of divorce on young minds resonates even more strongly in an era of increased focus on mental health and family dynamics. The way it depicts young people struggling with authenticity versus performance feels particularly prescient in our social media age.
