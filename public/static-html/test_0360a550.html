H2Omx dives deep into Mexico City's dire water crisis, following multiple narrative threads that weave together to create a comprehensive picture of one of the world's most pressing urban environmental challenges. The documentary tracks the journey of water from mountain springs through the city's complex infrastructure to its final destinations, revealing a system on the brink of collapse. Through intimate portraits of citizens from various social classes, the film illustrates how water access reflects and reinforces social inequality.
Directors Cohen and Hagerman employ a observational yet urgent visual style that recalls the environmental cinematography of Manufactured Landscapes (2006). Their camera captures both the massive scale of infrastructure and intimate moments of daily life affected by water scarcity. The film's structure masterfully balances technical information with human stories, similar to the approach used in An Inconvenient Truth (2006), but with a more immediate, ground-level perspective.
The documentary serves as a crucial wake-up call about urban water management and inequality, joining important environmental films like Watermark (2013) and Flow: For Love of Water (2008). It exposes how infrastructure decisions made decades ago continue to impact millions of lives today, while highlighting the intersection of environmental and social justice. The film's exploration of class divisions through water access echoes themes found in Even the Rain (2010).
Within the environmental documentary genre, H2Omx stands out for its focus on urban infrastructure rather than natural environments. It builds on the tradition of city-focused documentaries like Urbanized (2011) while incorporating the urgent environmental messaging of films like Chasing Ice (2012). The film's approach to complex systems recalls Last Call at the Oasis (2012), but with a tighter geographic focus that allows for deeper exploration.
As cities worldwide face increasing water stress due to climate change and population growth, H2Omx's examination of Mexico City serves as a crucial case study and warning. The film's themes of infrastructure inequality and environmental justice remain even more relevant today, echoing recent documentaries like Thirst (2004) and anticipating works like There's Something in the Water (2019).
