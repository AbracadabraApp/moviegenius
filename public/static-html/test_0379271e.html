Alice Tate, a wealthy Manhattan housewife, finds herself at a crossroads when she develops a crush on Joe, a handsome saxophone player. Seeking relief for back pain, she visits Dr. Yang, a mysterious herbalist who provides her with various magical potions that allow her to become invisible, fly, and reconnect with her past. Through these supernatural encounters, Alice begins questioning her privileged but empty lifestyle and the choices that led her to this point. The story weaves reality and fantasy in classic Woody Allen style, using magical realism to explore themes of self-discovery and personal transformation.
Mia Farrow shines in one of her most nuanced performances, capturing Alice's evolution from pampered socialite to self-aware woman with subtle grace. The supporting cast, including Joe Mantegna and William Hurt, provides solid emotional anchoring for Alice's fantastic journey. Allen's direction cleverly balances whimsy with sharp social observation, while Carlo Di Palma's cinematography gives Manhattan a dreamy, ethereal quality that enhances the film's magical elements.
The film serves as a pointed critique of upper-class Manhattan society, much like The Great Gatsby (1974) and Metropolitan (1990). It examines female identity and self-discovery in ways that echo An Unmarried Woman (1978), while its magical elements recall Bell, Book and Candle (1958). The story's exploration of privilege and personal awakening remains relevant to contemporary discussions about wealth inequality and feminine empowerment.
Allen's take on magical realism puts Alice in conversation with films like Juliet of the Spirits (1965) and later works such as Amélie (2001). The film bridges the gap between realistic character studies and fantasy, predating similar approaches in films like Midnight in Paris (2011) and The Purple Rose of Cairo (1985).
The film's examination of privilege, authenticity, and female self-actualization resonates strongly with contemporary audiences. Its critique of materialistic culture and empty social climbing feels particularly pointed in our current era of extreme wealth inequality and social media performance. The magical realist elements presage later works like Big Fish (2003) and The Life Aquatic with Steve Zissou (2004) in their use of fantasy to explore emotional truths.
