Following three young women trying to make it in Los Angeles, 'Girls' attempts to capture the zeitgeist of female empowerment in the early 1980s. The film tracks their intersecting lives through career struggles, romantic entanglements, and friendship dynamics. While it aims for the feminist spirit of films like An Unmarried Woman (1978), it lacks the depth and nuance necessary to truly resonate.
The lead performances from Buckman, D'Arbanville, and Gaybis show promise but are constrained by underwritten roles and dated stereotypes. Director Just Jaeckin, known for Emmanuelle (1974), brings his signature glossy style but struggles to find the right tone between comedy and drama. The result is a film that feels caught between exploitation and genuine commentary on women's experiences.
Released in 1980, the film arrived at a pivotal moment for women's representation in cinema, alongside more successful contemporaries like Private Benjamin (1980) and 9 to 5 (1980). While it attempts to tackle workplace sexism, relationship dynamics, and female friendship, its treatment of these themes feels superficial compared to its peers.
As a female ensemble comedy-drama, 'Girls' sits awkwardly between the provocative films of the 1970s and the emerging women's stories of the 1980s. While it shares DNA with films like Looking for Mr. Goodbar (1977), it lacks their dramatic weight. Its influence can be traced to later works, though films like Working Girl (1988) would handle similar themes with far more sophistication.
Modern viewers may find the film's portrayal of gender politics dated and problematic. While its core themes of female friendship and career ambition remain relevant, contemporary works like Bridesmaids (2011) and The Devil Wears Prada (2006) offer more nuanced and empowering takes on similar material.
