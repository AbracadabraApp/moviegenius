Set in Victorian England, the film follows William Adamson, a naturalist who loses everything in a shipwreck and finds himself dependent on the wealthy Alabaster family. He marries their daughter Eugenia and begins studying the local ant colonies, drawing parallel observations between insect and human society. The film builds with the precision of a Victorian novel, revealing dark family secrets beneath polite society's surface. Like The Remains of the Day (1993), the story masterfully peels back layers of propriety to expose raw human nature.
Mark Rylance delivers a nuanced performance as Adamson, capturing both scientific detachment and growing emotional turmoil. Director Philip Haas creates a visual language that seamlessly blends microscopic insect photography with human drama, reminiscent of The Elephant Man (1980) in its Victorian atmosphere and attention to period detail. The costume design becomes its own character, with patterns and colors echoing the insect world in a way that recalls The Age of Innocence (1993).
The film brilliantly examines Victorian class structure, scientific revolution, and sexual repression. Like The Piano (1993), it explores female sexuality within constraining social structures. The parallel between insect colonies and British aristocracy offers a biting critique of social hierarchies, while the naturalist's perspective challenges religious orthodoxy in ways that echo Creation (2009).
As a period drama, it transcends typical costume drama conventions by incorporating elements of psychological thriller and natural history documentary. Its approach to Victorian sexuality and science places it alongside films like Possession (2002) and The French Lieutenant's Woman (1981), while its examination of class and desire recalls The Go-Between (1971).
The film's exploration of scientific observation versus social pretense remains relevant to modern discussions about truth and appearance. Its themes of class mobility and sexual politics resonate with contemporary social debates, while its environmental observations feel prescient in our climate-conscious era. The way it links human and natural worlds anticipates later works like Upstream Color (2013).
