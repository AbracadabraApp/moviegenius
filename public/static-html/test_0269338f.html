The Way He Looks follows Leonardo, a blind teenager navigating high school life in São Paulo with his best friend Giovana. When Gabriel, a new student, arrives in their class, Leonardo experiences feelings he's never felt before. The film masterfully portrays Leonardo's journey of self-discovery, dealing with both his sexuality and his desire for independence from his overprotective parents. Through subtle touches and carefully constructed scenes, we experience the world as Leonardo does, making this much more than just another coming-of-age story.
Ghilherme Lobo's portrayal of Leonardo is remarkable in its authenticity, never falling into disability tropes. The chemistry between Lobo and Fabio Audi (Gabriel) feels genuine and unforced, reminiscent of the naturalistic performances in Call Me By Your Name (2017). Director Daniel Ribeiro's approach emphasizes sensory experiences beyond sight, creating an intimate perspective that draws viewers into Leonardo's world.
The film breaks new ground in LGBTQ+ cinema by intersecting disability and sexual identity without making either the sole focus of the narrative. Like Moonlight (2016), it treats its protagonist's journey with dignity and nuance. The film challenges both Brazilian and international cinema's traditional representations of disabled and LGBTQ+ characters, following the path of groundbreaking works like Pride (2014) and Milk (2008).
Within the coming-of-age genre, The Way He Looks stands alongside sensitive portrayals like Boys Don't Cry (1999) and Blue Is the Warmest Color (2013), while bringing its own unique perspective. The film's handling of first love recalls the tenderness of Beautiful Thing (1996), while its treatment of disability representation sets new standards for inclusive storytelling.
In an era where representation and authenticity are increasingly vital, The Way He Looks remains a benchmark for thoughtful, intersectional storytelling. Its themes of acceptance, independence, and self-discovery resonate with contemporary discussions about identity and inclusion, while its artistic merit stands alongside recent LGBTQ+ classics like Portrait of a Lady on Fire (2019).
