Based on Xaviera Hollander's bestselling memoir, 'The Happy Hooker' follows a Dutch immigrant who becomes New York's most famous madam. Lynn Redgrave portrays Xaviera, whose journey from secretary to sophisticated sex worker attempts to blend comedy with social commentary. The film chronicles her encounters with clients, law enforcement, and her evolution into a savvy businesswoman, though it often settles for broad comedy over meaningful character development.
Redgrave, fresh from more serious roles, brings an earnest charm to Xaviera but struggles with uneven material that can't decide between satire and exploitation. Director Nicholas Sgarro adopts a light touch that ultimately undermines any serious examination of the subject matter. The film's approach to sex work feels dated and superficial compared to more nuanced contemporaneous works like Taxi Driver (1976) or Klute (1971).
Released during the sexual revolution of the 1970s, the film reflects changing attitudes toward sexuality but fails to meaningfully engage with feminism or women's autonomy. Unlike Looking for Mr. Goodbar (1977) or An Unmarried Woman (1978), which tackled female sexuality with depth, 'The Happy Hooker' opts for titillation over substance. Its treatment of sex work feels particularly shallow compared to later films like Pretty Woman (1990) or Monster (2003).
The film sits uncomfortably between exploitation cinema and mainstream comedy, never fully committing to either. While contemporary films like Shampoo (1975) successfully blended sex and social satire, 'The Happy Hooker' lacks the wit or insight to elevate its material beyond surface-level entertainment. It's part of a wave of mid-70s films attempting to capitalize on changing sexual mores, but without the artistic ambition of works like The Last Tango in Paris (1972).
Modern viewers may find the film's approach to sex work problematic and dated. Unlike contemporary works such as Hustlers (2019) or series like 'The Deuce,' it fails to examine the complexities and power dynamics of the sex industry. Its lightweight treatment of serious subjects feels particularly tone-deaf in today's more nuanced discussions of sex work and gender politics.
