Rain follows Sadie Thompson, a free-spirited woman who becomes stranded on a South Pacific island alongside a group of passengers including the zealous missionary Alfred Davidson. The story unfolds as an intense psychological battle between Sadie's unapologetic lifestyle and Davidson's oppressive morality. As tropical rain pours endlessly, the confined setting becomes a pressure cooker for examining human nature, desire, and redemption.
Joan Crawford delivers one of her most daring pre-Code performances, shedding her flapper image to embody Sadie's complexity. Walter Huston's portrayal of Davidson masterfully reveals the character's dangerous self-righteousness. Lewis Milestone's direction emphasizes the claustrophobic atmosphere through innovative sound design, using the constant rain as both metaphor and psychological device. The film's technical achievements in early sound cinema remain impressive.
Released during the pre-Code era, Rain boldly tackled themes of sexuality, religious hypocrisy, and moral reformation that would soon be censored by the Hays Code. The film's frank treatment of these subjects makes it a crucial document of Hollywood's brief period of relative creative freedom, comparable to other boundary-pushing works like Baby Face (1933) and Red Dust (1932).
As an adaptation of W. Somerset Maugham's story 'Miss Thompson,' Rain exemplifies Hollywood's early sound-era ambition to bring sophisticated literary works to screen. Its influence can be traced through later psychological dramas like The Night of the Hunter (1955) and films exploring religious zealotry such as Elmer Gantry (1960).
The film's examination of moral authority, sexual politics, and social hypocrisy remains startlingly relevant. Its themes echo through modern works like Doubt (2008) and The Master (2012), while its critique of religious fundamentalism speaks to contemporary social debates.
