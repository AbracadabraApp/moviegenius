Set during the final year of the Spanish Civil War, The Devil's Backbone follows Carlos, a young boy left at an isolated orphanage housing the children of Republican loyalists. The institution harbors dark secrets, including the ghost of a young boy named Santi who mysteriously disappeared on the night an unexploded bomb landed in the courtyard. As Carlos uncovers the truth behind Santi's death, the film weaves together supernatural elements with the very real horrors of war and betrayal. The orphanage's staff, including the enigmatic Dr. Casares and headmistress Carmen, protect their charges while dealing with their own complex relationships and political allegiances.
Del Toro crafts a masterpiece of atmospheric storytelling, using the ghost story framework to explore deeper themes of innocence lost and historical trauma. The young cast delivers remarkably nuanced performances, while veterans Marisa Paredes and Federico Luppi bring gravitas to their roles as the orphanage's guardians. The director's signature visual style transforms the orphanage into a character itself, with amber-lit corridors and shadow-filled corners that echo the emotional weight of Spain's civil conflict.
The film stands as a powerful allegory for Spain's troubled past, using supernatural elements to process historical trauma. Like Pan's Labyrinth (2006), del Toro employs fantasy elements to illuminate harsh political realities. The unexploded bomb in the courtyard serves as a constant reminder of impending violence, while Santi's ghost represents the unresolved wounds of civil war. The film's exploration of childhood innocence amid political violence recalls works like Spirit of the Beehive (1973) and Au Revoir les Enfants (1987).
Del Toro elevates the ghost story genre by seamlessly blending supernatural horror with historical drama. Unlike traditional horror films that rely on jump scares, The Devil's Backbone creates tension through atmosphere and emotional investment, similar to The Others (2001) and The Orphanage (2007). The film helped establish del Toro's reputation for sophisticated horror that prioritizes character development and thematic depth over conventional scares.
The film's meditation on war's impact on children remains painfully relevant, while its exploration of institutional corruption and moral compromise speaks to contemporary political concerns. Its influence can be seen in modern horror-dramas like A Tale of Two Sisters (2003) and The Babadook (2014), which similarly use supernatural elements to explore psychological trauma.
