Happy Gilmore follows a failed hockey player who discovers his powerful slapshot technique translates to an unorthodox but effective golf swing. When his grandmother faces eviction, Happy joins the pro golf tour to earn money to save her house. The film brilliantly subverts golf's genteel traditions by injecting working-class fury and hockey violence into the country club atmosphere. Sandler crafts Happy into a uniquely sympathetic everyman hero - crude but well-intentioned, aggressive but ultimately driven by love for his grandmother.
This represents Adam Sandler at his most effectively balanced, before his comedy became more exaggerated in films like Little Nicky (2000). Christopher McDonald's portrayal of smug villain Shooter McGavin provides the perfect foil, while Carl Weathers brings legitimacy as Happy's mentor. The film succeeds by maintaining just enough sports movie authenticity amid the comedy, similar to how Caddyshack (1980) balanced golf culture satire with slapstick.
Happy Gilmore cleverly tackles class warfare within golf's elitist culture, positioning Happy as a blue-collar hero infiltrating and disrupting the country club set. The film follows the tradition of sports comedies like Major League (1989) and The Bad News Bears (1976) in championing underdogs against institutional snobbery. Its enduring popularity speaks to audiences' continued appetite for seeing privileged systems humorously upended.
The film smartly combines elements of classic sports underdog stories like Rocky (1976) with the irreverent comedy style emerging in the 1990s. It helped establish the template for sports comedies like Talladega Nights (2006) and Blades of Glory (2007) - taking a sport seriously enough to ground the story while mining its culture for comedy. The movie's influence can be seen in later works like The Waterboy (1998) and Dodgeball (2004).
While some 90s comedies have aged poorly, Happy Gilmore's core themes of fighting corporate greed and standing up to elitism remain relevant. Its quotable lines and memorable scenes continue to resonate in pop culture, referenced everywhere from actual golf tournaments to social media. The film's blend of heart and humor provides a template for modern sports comedies that aim to be both entertaining and emotionally engaging.
