Based on the infamous 1950 Brink's robbery in Boston, the film follows small-time crook Tony Pino (Peter Falk) who masterminds what would become the largest robbery in U.S. history at that time. The beauty of the story lies in its portrayal of working-class criminals who stumble upon the heist of a lifetime. Falk brings a compelling mix of street smarts and vulnerability to Pino, making him more of an opportunistic everyman than a hardened criminal. The crew's meticulous planning and execution of the robbery becomes a testament to ingenuity rather than violence.
William Friedkin, fresh off his successes with The Exorcist (1973) and The French Connection (1971), brings his characteristic attention to detail and gritty realism to this period piece. Peter Falk, known for his iconic role in Columbo, flips brilliantly to the other side of the law, while Peter Boyle delivers a standout supporting performance. Friedkin's direction emphasizes the workmanlike nature of the crew, avoiding the glamorous heist movie tropes seen in films like Ocean's 11 (1960).
The film captures the post-WWII era in Boston perfectly, showing a time when returning veterans and working-class citizens faced limited opportunities in a changing economy. It shares thematic DNA with other period crime films like Miller's Crossing (1990) and The Sting (1973), examining how economic disparity breeds creative criminality. The film's exploration of class warfare and institutional corruption resonates with audiences even today.
While released in the late 1970s, The Brink's Job stands apart from the decade's grittier crime films like Mean Streets (1973) or Dog Day Afternoon (1975). Instead, it harks back to earlier heist films like The Asphalt Jungle (1950), focusing on the mechanics of the crime and the human drama rather than excessive violence. This approach influenced later films like Going in Style (1979) and The Old Man & the Gun (2018).
Modern audiences will find surprising parallels between this historical heist and contemporary true-crime narratives. The film's examination of security vulnerabilities and institutional overconfidence remains relevant in our digital age. Its influence can be seen in recent heist films like American Animals (2018) and The Town (2010), which similarly explore the human motivations behind spectacular robberies.
