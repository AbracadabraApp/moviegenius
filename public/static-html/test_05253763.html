In this technicolor noir, Ben Grace (John Payne) is a shrewd operator working for a crime boss who plots to take over the syndicate. He becomes entangled with two sisters: Dorothy (Rhonda Fleming), a straight-laced secretary to a reform politician, and June (Arlene Dahl), her kleptomaniac sister fresh out of prison. The psychological complexity between the sisters drives the narrative as Ben manipulates both women while pursuing his own agenda. The film's exploration of family loyalty against criminal ambition creates a uniquely charged atmosphere.
Director Allan Dwan, a veteran of the silent era, brings noir sensibilities into vivid color, working with legendary cinematographer John Alton to create a distinctive visual palette that challenges noir conventions. Fleming and Dahl deliver nuanced performances as the contrasting sisters, while Payne perfectly embodies the morally ambiguous antihero typical of noir. The film's use of color emphasizes psychological states rather than mere visual spectacle.
Released during the decline of classic noir, 'Slightly Scarlet' represents a fascinating transition point in the genre. Its exploration of criminal corruption in local politics resonated with contemporary concerns about urban crime and governance. The film's treatment of female characters, particularly its complex portrayal of sisterhood and psychological dysfunction, was ahead of its time.
While most noir films embraced black and white photography, 'Slightly Scarlet' proves color can enhance noir themes. It follows the tradition of Leave Her to Heaven (1945) in using vibrant Technicolor to explore dark psychological territory. The film's influence can be seen in later color noirs like Vertigo (1958) and modern neo-noirs such as Body Heat (1981).
The film's examination of political corruption and criminal influence in local government remains relevant today. Its psychological complexity and visual innovation continue to influence modern filmmakers, particularly in how color can be used to express noir themes, as seen in films like Drive (2011) and Blade Runner 2049 (2017).
