Roger Willoughby (Rock Hudson) is a fishing equipment salesman who's built his reputation as an expert angler - with one small problem: he's never actually fished in his life. When PR woman Abigail Page (Paula Prentiss) ropes him into participating in a fishing tournament, his carefully constructed facade begins to unravel. The premise brilliantly sets up classic screwball situations where maintaining appearances leads to increasingly complicated deceptions.
Howard Hawks revisits themes from his earlier masterpiece Bringing Up Baby (1938), crafting another tale of a straight-laced man whose life is upended by a free-spirited woman. Hudson channels Cary Grant's gift for physical comedy, while Prentiss brings a refreshing modernness to the classic Hawks heroine archetype. Their height difference creates visual comedy reminiscent of Pillow Talk (1959), another Hudson romantic comedy.
The film cleverly subverts 1960s masculinity, questioning the authenticity of male expertise and authority. Released when second-wave feminism was gaining momentum, it presents a competent female character who sees through male posturing. The comedy stems from deflating male ego rather than female stereotypes, making it surprisingly progressive for its era.
While screwball comedy peaked in the 1930s with films like The Lady Eve (1941), Hawks demonstrates the genre's continuing vitality in the 1960s. The film bridges classic screwball traditions with the emerging sexual revolution era romantic comedies like Send Me No Flowers (1964) and Sex and the Single Girl (1964).
The theme of impostor syndrome and maintaining false expertise resonates strongly today, particularly in our social media age where perceived expertise often trumps actual experience. The film's gentle mockery of masculinity and authority feels particularly relevant amid contemporary discussions of toxic masculinity and authentic identity.
