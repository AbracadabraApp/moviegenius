In this delightful fish-out-of-water comedy, Charles Laughton plays Marmaduke Ruggles, a proper English butler who finds himself 'lost' in a poker game to nouveau riche American couple the Flouds. Transplanted from Paris to the American frontier town of Red Gap, Washington, Ruggles must navigate a culture that challenges everything he knows about social hierarchy and proper behavior. The film's genius lies in watching Laughton's character gradually embrace American democratic ideals while maintaining his essential dignity.
Charles Laughton, known primarily for dramatic roles like The Private Life of Henry VIII (1933), delivers a masterclass in subtle comedy. Director Leo McCarey, who would later helm Duck Soup (1933) and The Awful Truth (1937), brings his signature touch for mixing humor with genuine human emotion. The supporting cast, particularly Mary Boland and Charlie Ruggles as the wealthy American couple, provides perfect foils for Laughton's transformation.
The film brilliantly subverts expectations about class and democracy, using comedy to explore serious themes about American values. The famous scene where Ruggles recites the Gettysburg Address in a saloon remains one of cinema's most moving statements about democracy and human dignity. Made during the Great Depression, the film's optimistic view of American social mobility and individual worth resonated deeply with audiences.
While contemporary comedies like It Happened One Night (1934) and My Man Godfrey (1936) explored class differences through romantic relationships, 'Ruggles' takes a unique approach by focusing on personal transformation through cultural exchange. The film influenced later fish-out-of-water comedies like The King and I (1956) and Trading Places (1983), establishing many tropes of the genre.
The film's exploration of class barriers, cultural adaptation, and human dignity remains startlingly relevant. Modern viewers will recognize themes that echo in contemporary discussions about immigration, class mobility, and American identity. The humor remains fresh and the emotional core genuine, making it far more than just a historical curiosity.
