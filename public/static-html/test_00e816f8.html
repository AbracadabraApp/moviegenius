This ambitious but flawed retelling of Columbus's voyage to the Americas suffers from a simplistic narrative approach. The story follows Christopher Columbus (Georges Corraface) as he attempts to convince the Spanish crown to fund his expedition westward. Despite the rich historical material, the film reduces complex historical figures to one-dimensional characters, with Marlon Brando's disappointing turn as Torquemada feeling particularly phoned-in. The journey itself, which should be the film's highlight, lacks both the necessary tension and historical gravity.
Director John Glen, known for his work on James Bond films like Licence to Kill (1989), struggles to bring the required gravitas to this historical epic. The performances range from mediocre to bewildering, with even the usually reliable Marlon Brando seeming disinterested. Georges Corraface's Columbus lacks the charisma and complexity needed to carry such an ambitious narrative. The film's visual approach, while occasionally striking in its seabound sequences, fails to capture the epic scope achieved by similar historical adventures like Master and Commander: The Far Side of the World (2003).
Released during the 500th anniversary of Columbus's voyage, the film notably fails to engage with the more problematic aspects of European colonization, offering instead a sanitized version of events that feels dated even for 1992. Unlike more nuanced historical epics like The Mission (1986) or The New World (2005), this production glosses over the complex cultural interactions and devastating consequences of Columbus's discovery. Its simplistic approach to history stands in stark contrast to modern historical dramas that tackle colonial subjects with greater awareness and sensitivity.
Within the historical adventure genre, this film falls short of contemporaries like Dances with Wolves (1990) and predecessors like The Last Valley (1971). It attempts to follow in the tradition of grand historical epics like Ben-Hur (1959) but lacks both the narrative sophistication and production values to achieve such ambitions. The film represents a particular moment in 1990s cinema when historical accuracy began to take precedence over pure entertainment in historical epics, though it succeeds at neither.
Modern viewers will likely find the film's portrayal of historical events problematic and oversimplified. In an era where historical narratives are being rightfully reexamined and retold from multiple perspectives, this film's straightforward glorification of Columbus feels particularly out of touch. Contemporary films like Apocalypto (2006) and The Lost City of Z (2016) demonstrate how historical adventures can maintain entertainment value while engaging more thoughtfully with complex historical realities.
