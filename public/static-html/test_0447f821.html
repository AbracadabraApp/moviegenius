Goona Goona presents a romanticized tale of forbidden love in Bali, following a prince who falls for a commoner against social customs. The film blends staged dramatic sequences with documentary footage of Balinese rituals and daily life, creating a hybrid format that would influence later exploitation films. The term 'goona-goona' refers to alleged love potions and magical practices, which the film sensationalizes for Western audiences.
The film features non-professional Balinese actors in leading roles, with performances that feel stilted and directed through a distinctly colonial lens. Roosevelt and Denis's direction emphasizes exotic elements while attempting to create a narrative structure that would appeal to Western audiences. The documentary portions showcase authentic ceremonial dances and rituals, though these are often taken out of context.
Goona Goona emerged during Hollywood's pre-Code era when exotic exploitation films drew curious audiences. It sparked a brief 'goona-goona' exploitation cycle in the 1930s, influencing films like Virgins of Bali (1932) and Bird of Paradise (1932). The film represents problematic colonial attitudes and the Western fascination with 'primitive' cultures, similar to how Nanook of the North (1922) portrayed Inuit life through a Western perspective.
The film sits at an intersection between documentary and exploitation cinema, predating later ethno-exploitation films like Mondo Cane (1962). Its approach influenced the 'exotic documentary' genre, though modern viewers recognize its cultural appropriation and misrepresentation. The film's technique of mixing authentic footage with staged scenes would become a template for numerous exploitation documentaries.
Today, Goona Goona serves as a historical document of both Balinese culture and Western colonial attitudes. Its problematic aspects provide important lessons about cultural representation and exploitation in cinema, similar to discussions surrounding films like King Kong (1933) and The Good Earth (1937). The film raises crucial questions about documentary ethics and cultural appropriation that remain relevant to modern filmmaking.
