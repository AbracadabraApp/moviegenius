Set in early 18th-century England, The Favourite demolishes stuffy period piece conventions with a deliciously twisted tale of power, manipulation, and desire. Queen Anne (Olivia Colman) rules England while struggling with her health and confidence. Her closest friend and secret lover, Lady Sarah (Rachel Weisz), effectively governs the country until her cousin Abigail (Emma Stone) arrives at court. What follows is a ruthless battle for the Queen's affections, with both women employing increasingly devious tactics to secure their position as the favorite.
Lanthimos crafts a masterwork of performance, with Colman delivering an Oscar-winning portrayal that switches between vulnerability and tyranny with heartbreaking precision. Stone and Weisz match her beat for beat, creating a triangle of manipulation that crackles with tension and dark humor. The director's signature style - using fish-eye lenses and candlelit scenes - creates a claustrophobic, off-kilter world that perfectly mirrors the story's themes of power and perspective.
The film brilliantly subverts gender roles and period drama expectations, showing women wielding real power in a male-dominated society. Unlike traditional costume dramas that focus on romance and propriety, The Favourite exposes the raw ambition and sexuality beneath the powdered wigs and elaborate costumes. It's a sharp commentary on how power corrupts and the personal cost of political ambition.
While it shares DNA with classic court dramas like Barry Lyndon (1975) and The Madness of King George (1994), The Favourite reinvents the genre with modern sensibilities and dark humor reminiscent of All About Eve (1950). Its influence can be seen in subsequent historical dramas like Mary Queen of Scots (2018) and The Great (2020), which embrace similar irreverent approaches to history.
The film's exploration of political manipulation, female agency, and the personal cost of power remains startlingly relevant. Its themes of favoritism and court politics mirror contemporary political dynamics, while its treatment of sexuality and power relationships feels thoroughly modern despite its historical setting.
