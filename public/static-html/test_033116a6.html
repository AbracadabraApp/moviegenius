Set against the backdrop of World War I Italy, this adaptation follows American ambulance driver Frederick Henry (Rock Hudson) who falls in love with British nurse Catherine Barkley (Jennifer Jones). Their passionate romance unfolds amid the chaos of war, leading to desertion, pregnancy, and ultimately tragedy. While the film maintains the basic framework of Hemingway's story, it emphasizes romantic melodrama over the novel's brutal war commentary.
Rock Hudson and Jennifer Jones lack the raw chemistry needed to sell this epic romance. Their performances often feel staged rather than lived-in, unlike the authenticity found in similar war romances like Casablanca (1942). Director Charles Vidor opts for a glossy CinemaScope treatment that, while visually impressive, undermines the gritty realism of Hemingway's vision. The film's technicolor grandeur works against the story's intimate nature.
Released during the Cold War, this version softens Hemingway's harsh anti-war stance to align with 1950s Hollywood conventions. Where the novel offered a scathing critique of military glory, the film transforms the material into a more conventional love story. This reflects the era's tendency to romanticize World War I, as seen in films like The Bridge on the River Kwai (1957).
While war romances like From Here to Eternity (1953) successfully balanced love and combat, this adaptation struggles to find that equilibrium. It follows a trend of 1950s literary adaptations that prioritized spectacle over substance, similar to The Sun Also Rises (1957), another compromised Hemingway adaptation from the same year.
Modern viewers may find this version dated compared to more nuanced war romances like The English Patient (1996) or Atonement (2007). However, it serves as an interesting case study in how Hollywood's approach to war stories evolved from the 1930s to the 1950s, particularly in how combat trauma and romance were depicted on screen.
