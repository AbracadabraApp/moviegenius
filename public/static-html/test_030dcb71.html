Set in an English village during WWII, 'Great Day' follows the preparations for Eleanor Roosevelt's visit to the local Women's Institute. The story centers on Margaret Ellis, played by Flora Robson, who leads the Institute while dealing with personal struggles involving her daughter and husband. The film attempts to capture the spirit of wartime Britain through the microcosm of village life, though it sometimes falls into predictable patterns of the era's propaganda-tinged storytelling.
Flora Robson delivers a commanding performance as Margaret, bringing dignity and depth to what could have been a one-dimensional role. Director Lance Comfort's vision emphasizes the communal spirit of wartime Britain, though his approach often feels stagey by modern standards. The film's greatest strength lies in its ensemble cast's ability to convey the quiet determination of civilians during wartime.
The film represents a fascinating snapshot of British wartime cinema, highlighting the role of women's organizations in maintaining home front morale. It shares themes with Mrs. Miniver (1942) and The Way to the Stars (1945), depicting how ordinary citizens adapted to extraordinary circumstances. The Eleanor Roosevelt plot device serves to underscore Anglo-American relations during WWII.
Within the context of 1940s British cinema, 'Great Day' sits alongside works like Millions Like Us (1943) and This Happy Breed (1944) in portraying everyday heroism. However, it lacks the emotional complexity found in contemporaneous works like Brief Encounter (1945) or the technical innovation of The Way Ahead (1944).
While the film's depiction of community organization and women's leadership remains relevant, its storytelling approach feels antiquated. Modern viewers may find more resonance in later examinations of wartime Britain like Hope and Glory (1987) or The King's Speech (2010), which tackle similar themes with greater nuance.
