Manpower follows the dangerous lives of high-voltage power line workers, focusing on Johnny Marshall (Edward G. Robinson) and his best friend Hank McHenry (George Raft). Their friendship is tested when both men fall for Fay Duval (Marlene Dietrich), a world-weary nightclub worker with a troubled past. The film masterfully weaves together workplace danger, romantic tension, and male friendship, creating a compelling narrative that explores loyalty and betrayal against the backdrop of a hazardous profession.
Robinson delivers a nuanced performance as the veteran lineman, while Dietrich brings her signature blend of glamour and world-weariness to Fay. Raft's portrayal of the younger, more impulsive Hank creates perfect dramatic tension. Walsh's direction emphasizes both the physical dangers of the men's work and the emotional perils of their personal lives, creating a gritty authenticity that would later influence post-war noir films.
Released just before America's entry into World War II, the film captures a crucial moment in American labor history, celebrating the working class while exploring themes of masculinity and sacrifice. The dangerous profession of power line work serves as a metaphor for the risks men take both in their careers and personal lives, reflecting the era's changing dynamics of gender roles and workplace danger.
While predating classic film noir, Manpower contains many elements that would become noir staples: the femme fatale character, male friendship strained by romance, and dangerous professions. It bridges the gap between 1930s working-class dramas like They Drive by Night (1940) and the post-war noir style of White Heat (1949).
The film's exploration of workplace safety, male friendship, and romantic rivalry remains relevant today. Its portrayal of essential workers risking their lives resonates particularly well in our current era, while the complex relationship dynamics and moral ambiguity feel thoroughly modern.
