This controversial exploitation film cobbles together recycled footage of Bruce Lee with new material shot after his death, attempting to create a narrative around a martial arts championship. The film notoriously uses clips from an old Hong Kong film where Lee played a supporting role, mixing it with documentary-style footage and newly shot scenes with other martial artists. The result is a confusing mashup that tries to capitalize on Lee's posthumous fame.
The film's approach to using Bruce Lee footage is problematic and deceptive. The new footage features Fred Williamson and Ron Van Clief as themselves, participating in interview segments that attempt to add legitimacy to the project. The editing creates a disjointed experience that fails to honor Lee's legacy or provide genuine martial arts entertainment.
This film represents the darker side of Bruce Lee's impact on cinema - the rush to profit from his fame after his death in 1973. Similar to films like Game of Death (1978) and The Last Dragon (1985), it exemplifies the 'Bruceploitation' genre that emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s. These films often used look-alikes and recycled footage to capitalize on Lee's popularity.
While legitimate martial arts films like The Chinese Connection (1972) and Five Fingers of Death (1972) were establishing the genre's credibility, exploitation films like this one threatened to undermine it. The contrast between authentic martial arts cinema and cash-grab productions became particularly stark during this era.
Today, the film serves as a cautionary tale about exploitation in cinema and the commodification of deceased stars. Its techniques seem especially crude compared to modern approaches to posthumous appearances, like those in Furious 7 (2015) with Paul Walker or Rogue One (2016) with Peter Cushing.
