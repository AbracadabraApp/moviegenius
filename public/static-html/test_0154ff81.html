The film follows Nilkantha Bagchi, an alcoholic intellectual played by Ghatak himself, wandering through a politically turbulent Calcutta. Through his encounters with various characters, including a young woman named Nachiketa and several political activists, the film weaves a complex narrative about displacement, disillusionment, and the search for meaning in a fractured society. The protagonist's journey mirrors Ghatak's own struggles with alcoholism and his deep anguish over the partition of Bengal.
Ghatak's performance as Nilkantha is raw and autobiographical, bringing an uncomfortable authenticity to the role. His direction employs unconventional techniques, using fragmented narrative structures and symbolic imagery to convey the psychological turmoil of his characters. The film's visual style, with its use of wide-angle lenses and expressionistic compositions, creates a sense of displacement that perfectly matches its themes.
Made during a period of intense political upheaval in West Bengal, the film captures the zeitgeist of 1970s India, particularly the Naxalite movement and its impact on Bengali society. It serves as both a personal confession and a broader commentary on the intellectual class's response to social revolution. The film's exploration of displacement and cultural identity resonates with other partition narratives like Meghe Dhaka Tara (1960) and Subarnarekha (1965).
As a key work of Indian Parallel Cinema, the film shares thematic concerns with contemporaneous works like Ankur (1974) and Bhuvan Shome (1969). Its experimental narrative structure and political engagement place it alongside other revolutionary works of world cinema like La Chinoise (1967) and Hour of the Furnaces (1968).
The film's examination of intellectual crisis and social responsibility remains startlingly relevant today. Its questioning of the role of art and artists in times of political upheaval echoes contemporary debates about artistic engagement with social issues. The personal-political dialectic it explores continues to influence filmmakers like Mani Kaul and Kumar Shahani.
