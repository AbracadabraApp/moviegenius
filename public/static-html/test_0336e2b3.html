Plastic China follows the lives of two families in a small recycling workshop in China, where imported plastic waste becomes their economic lifeline. The film centers on 11-year-old Yi-Jie, who learns about the outside world through the plastic waste she sorts while dreaming of attending school. Her story intertwines with Kun, the facility owner who struggles between providing for his family and dealing with the toxic reality of their work.
Director Wang's observational approach creates an intimate portrait that recalls the raw authenticity of Last Train Home (2009). The camera becomes nearly invisible as it captures candid moments of both hardship and hope. Wang's patient documentation style allows complex relationships to unfold naturally, creating a narrative that feels both personal and universal.
The film exposes the human cost of global recycling practices, joining important environmental documentaries like An Inconvenient Truth (2006) and Waste Land (2010). It challenges viewers to confront their own consumption habits while highlighting the interconnected nature of global waste management. The documentary sparked significant discussion in China about waste import policies, contributing to the country's eventual ban on plastic waste imports.
Within the eco-documentary genre, Plastic China stands alongside works like Manufactured Landscapes (2006) and The True Cost (2015) in its examination of globalization's environmental impact. However, it distinguishes itself by focusing on intimate human stories rather than statistics, similar to how Up the Yangtze (2007) personalized China's Three Gorges Dam project.
As global plastic consumption continues to rise, this film remains critically relevant. It connects to contemporary discussions of environmental justice, global trade, and economic inequality. The story resonates with recent documentaries like The Story of Plastic (2019) and provides crucial context for understanding current recycling crises and policy changes.
