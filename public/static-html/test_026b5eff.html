Based on the notorious 1988 Junko Furuta case, this controversial film follows the horrific true story of a high school girl's abduction and torture by four teenage boys. The film attempts to examine the psychological state of Japan's youth in the late 1980s, but its approach remains problematic. The narrative focuses on the perpetrators' descent into extreme violence, while attempting to contextualize their actions within the broader scope of societal pressures and juvenile delinquency.
Director Katsuya Matsumura takes a raw, unflinching approach that mirrors the stark documentary style of films like A (1998) and Kids (1995). The young cast delivers intense performances that capture the psychological deterioration of their characters, though the film's exploitation elements often overshadow any deeper character study. The documentary-style cinematography creates an uncomfortable immediacy that serves the subject matter but sometimes feels needlessly graphic.
The film emerged during Japan's examination of youth violence in the 1990s, alongside works like Battle Royale (2000) and Love & Pop (1998). While it attempts to address serious issues of juvenile crime, bullying, and societal pressure, its sensationalistic approach undermines any meaningful commentary. The film reflects the period's moral panic about youth culture but fails to offer substantive insights into the root causes of such violence.
Within the true crime genre, this film stands as a controversial entry that raises questions about the ethics of dramatizing real-life tragedies. Unlike more thoughtful examinations like The Snowtown Murders (2011) or United 93 (2006), it struggles to find the right balance between documentation and exploitation. Its approach influenced later Japanese extreme cinema but often for problematic reasons.
The film's themes of youth alienation and violence remain relevant, but modern audiences would be better served by more nuanced examinations like We Need to Talk About Kevin (2011) or Blue Spring (2001). Its handling of true crime material serves as a cautionary tale about the responsibilities of filmmakers when dealing with real tragedies.
