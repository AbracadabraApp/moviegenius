Little Forest follows Hye-won, a young woman who retreats from Seoul's competitive urban life to her rural childhood home after failing her teaching certification exam. Through four seasons, she reconnects with herself by growing, cooking, and sharing food, while processing her complicated relationship with her absent mother. The deliberate pacing mirrors the natural rhythms of farming and cooking, creating an immersive experience that feels like a warm embrace.
Kim Tae-ri, fresh from her breakthrough in The Handmaiden (2016), brings a quiet determination to Hye-won, making every small victory in the kitchen or garden feel momentous. Director Yim Soon-rye crafts an intimate portrait that recalls the meditative quality of Still Walking (2008), while bringing her own feminist perspective to this story of female independence and self-discovery. The film's approach to food preparation scenes rivals Tampopo (1985) in its ability to make cooking feel like a transcendent act.
The film taps into the growing 'escape from urban life' movement in Asia, similar to the themes explored in Last Letter (2018) and A Simple Life (2011). It addresses contemporary issues of youth unemployment, urban alienation, and the search for authenticity in an increasingly disconnected world. The mother-daughter relationship echoes complex family dynamics seen in Shoplifters (2018).
Within the 'healing cinema' genre popular in East Asia, Little Forest stands out for its lack of melodrama and authentic approach to personal growth. Unlike Eat Drink Man Woman (1994), it uses food not as a metaphor for family tension, but as a direct path to self-nurturing. The film's seasonal structure recalls Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter... and Spring (2003), though with a more grounded, contemporary perspective.
In an era of burnout culture and digital overwhelm, Little Forest offers a timely reminder about the importance of slowing down and reconnecting with our food, environment, and ourselves. Its themes of sustainable living and finding purpose outside traditional success metrics resonate strongly with millennials questioning urban-centric lifestyles, especially post-pandemic.
