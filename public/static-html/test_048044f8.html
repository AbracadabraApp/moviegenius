Stavisky follows the final months of Serge Alexandre Stavisky, a Ukrainian-born Jewish financier and embezzler who scandalized French society in the 1930s. Belmondo portrays Stavisky as a charming swindler living a luxurious life through elaborate financial schemes, managing theaters and pawning fake jewels while moving through elite social circles. The film captures his double life as both 'Alexandre,' the respected financier, and his true identity as Stavisky, a man with a criminal past trying to outrun his destiny.
Resnais crafts a sophisticated portrait of 1930s France with the same innovative approach he brought to Hiroshima Mon Amour (1959). Belmondo delivers one of his most nuanced performances, departing from his action-hero persona to embody Stavisky's complexity. The film's elegant visual style, complemented by Sondheim's first film score, creates a melancholic atmosphere that prefigures the character's inevitable downfall.
The Stavisky Affair, which the film chronicles, contributed to the political crisis that shook France's Third Republic. Like Z (1969) and The Conformist (1970), Stavisky examines how personal corruption intersects with political upheaval. The film's exploration of antisemitism and financial fraud in pre-war Europe remains particularly relevant to contemporary discussions about economic inequality and social prejudice.
Stavisky represents a sophisticated evolution of the biographical drama, following in the tradition of The Battle of Algiers (1966) while anticipating later political dramas like Il Divo (2008). Resnais blends historical accuracy with artistic license, creating a film that's both historically informative and dramatically compelling, similar to how Army of Shadows (1969) approached French Resistance history.
Modern viewers will find striking parallels between Stavisky's financial schemes and contemporary financial scandals. The film's examination of how charismatic figures can manipulate social and financial systems echoes in recent works like The Wolf of Wall Street (2013) and Inside Job (2010), while its exploration of political corruption remains eternally relevant.
