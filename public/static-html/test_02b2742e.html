Prem Ratan Dhan Payo follows the classic prince-and-pauper narrative with a distinctly Indian twist. Salman Khan plays dual roles: Prince Vijay, a stern ruler estranged from his family, and Prem Dilwale, a charming theater actor who becomes his lookalike replacement after an assassination attempt. The story explores themes of identity, family reconciliation, and love through elaborate set pieces and emotional confrontations. Like The King and I (1956), it uses the royal setting to examine class differences and social transformation.
Director Sooraj Barjatya returns to his signature style of family-oriented storytelling, reminiscent of his earlier work Hum Aapke Hain Koun..! (1994). Salman Khan delivers one of his most nuanced performances, effectively distinguishing between the two characters while maintaining audience engagement. Sonam Kapoor as Princess Maithili brings grace to her role, creating chemistry that recalls classic pairs like in Mughal-e-Azam (1960).
The film represents a conscious attempt to bridge traditional Indian values with contemporary sensibilities, much like Kabhi Khushi Kabhie Gham (2001). It addresses modern family dynamics while preserving the grandeur of Indian cultural traditions. The elaborate musical numbers and family ceremonies serve as more than mere spectacle, they become vehicles for emotional storytelling and cultural preservation.
Within the context of 2010s Bollywood, PRDP stands as one of the last true big-budget family dramas in the traditional mold, following the path of films like Devdas (2002) and Baghban (2003). While contemporary Indian cinema increasingly embraced grittier, more realistic narratives, this film proudly maintains the opulent, idealistic storytelling style that defined the 1990s golden era of family entertainers.
Despite its traditional framework, the film's messages about family unity, forgiveness, and personal transformation remain relevant. Like The Crown (2016) series, it uses royal drama to explore universal family dynamics. The film's emphasis on reconciliation and understanding across generational gaps speaks to modern audiences facing similar family challenges in an increasingly fragmented social landscape.
