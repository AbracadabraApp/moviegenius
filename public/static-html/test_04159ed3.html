In the Shadows follows Trojan, a professional criminal recently released from prison who immediately returns to his old life of heists and double-crosses. The film's spare narrative focuses on his methodical preparation for a gold heist while navigating betrayals from former associates. Like the protagonists of Le Samouraï (1967) and Drive (2011), Trojan is a man of few words but precise actions, moving through Berlin's criminal underworld with calculated determination.
Matičević's performance as Trojan is a masterclass in minimalist acting, every gesture and glance carrying weight. Arslan's direction eschews typical crime thriller theatrics for a controlled, observational style that builds tension through stillness rather than action. The approach recalls Michael Mann's Thief (1981) in its focus on professional methodology and urban alienation.
The film reflects post-reunification Berlin's transformation, depicting a city caught between its divided past and globalized present. Like The American Friend (1977), it uses the crime genre to explore German identity in an increasingly borderless Europe. The criminal underworld becomes a metaphor for capitalism's shadow economy, with loyalty and trust as commodities to be traded.
In the Shadows represents a distinctly European take on neo-noir, following in the tradition of The Beat That My Heart Skipped (2005) and The Square (2008). It strips away genre excess to focus on character and atmosphere, creating tension through observation rather than action. This approach aligns it with the Berlin School of filmmaking's emphasis on realism and psychological complexity.
The film's themes of surveillance, trust, and professional isolation feel especially relevant in our digital age. Its portrayal of a man trying to maintain personal codes in an increasingly corrupt world echoes contemporary concerns about privacy and institutional power, similar to films like A Most Wanted Man (2014).
