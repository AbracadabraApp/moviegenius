The Lords of Salem follows Heidi Hawthorne, a radio DJ in Salem, Massachusetts who receives a mysterious vinyl record from 'The Lords.' When played, the record triggers disturbing visions and a descent into madness connected to Salem's dark history of witch trials. The film masterfully builds tension through Heidi's gradual psychological deterioration, reminiscent of Rosemary's Baby (1968) in its portrayal of a woman's isolation and paranoia.
Sheri Moon Zombie delivers her most nuanced performance as Heidi, showing vulnerability rarely seen in her previous collaborations with director Rob Zombie. The film marks a significant stylistic evolution for Zombie, abandoning his typical grindhouse aesthetics for a more atmospheric approach inspired by European horror classics like Suspiria (1977). The dreamy, hallucinatory sequences echo the work of Ken Russell, particularly The Devils (1971).
The film explores themes of generational trauma and historical guilt, using Salem's witch trials as a backdrop to examine how past atrocities echo into the present. Like The Witch (2015), it connects historical witch persecution to modern female oppression. The movie's commentary on mass hysteria and religious fundamentalism remains particularly relevant in our current climate of social division.
Within the witch-horror subgenre, The Lords of Salem stands out for its art-house approach, bridging the gap between exploitation cinema and elevated horror. It shares DNA with films like Kill List (2011) and A Field in England (2013) in its psychedelic approach to folk horror, while its surreal finale recalls Mother! (2017) in its bold, divisive imagery.
The film's themes of addiction, recovery, and societal persecution resonate strongly with contemporary discussions about mental health and social stigma. Its experimental nature and willingness to challenge audience expectations aligns with the current wave of ambitious horror films like Hereditary (2018) and Midsommar (2019).
