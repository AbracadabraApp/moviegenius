Double Tide follows a woman clamdigger in coastal Maine who works during both low tides of a single day - at dawn and dusk. The film consists of two extended takes, each about 45 minutes long, capturing the solitary work of harvesting clams in the mudflats. Through its patient observation, we witness the physical demands of this traditional profession and the meditative quality of working in sync with natural rhythms.
Director Sharon Lockhart's vision exemplifies the power of observational cinema, recalling the methodical approach of James Benning's 13 Lakes (2004) and Ten Skies (2004). The 'performance' here is the actual labor of the protagonist, Jen Casad, whose movements become a choreography of work, reminiscent of the attention to physical labor seen in La Libertad (2001).
The film serves as a crucial document of traditional labor practices in modern America, joining works like Leviathan (2012) and Sweetgrass (2009) in examining human interaction with nature through labor. It raises questions about sustainability, traditional ways of life, and the relationship between work and natural cycles in an increasingly industrialized world.
Double Tide positions itself within the tradition of structural film and slow cinema, alongside works like Sleep (1963) and Empire (1964) by Andy Warhol. Its formal rigor and attention to duration challenge conventional documentary storytelling, aligning with contemporary slow cinema masterworks like At Sea (2007).
In an era of increasing automation and disconnection from natural processes, Double Tide's meditation on manual labor and environmental rhythms becomes increasingly relevant. It shares concerns with recent environmental documentaries like Anthropocene: The Human Epoch (2018), while maintaining its distinct observational approach.
