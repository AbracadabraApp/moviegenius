Benjamin Purvis, a home-schooled teenager with dreams of becoming a fantasy author, attends a writers' camp where his manuscript catches the attention of his idol, the pompous sci-fi author Dr. Ronald Chevalier. When Chevalier plagiarizes Benjamin's story, it sets off a bizarre chain of events involving low-budget film adaptations, yeast-based pharmaceuticals, and surreal fantasy sequences featuring Sam Rockwell in dual roles as the story's protagonist.
Jemaine Clement delivers the film's standout performance as the pretentious Chevalier, channeling a delicious mix of literary snob and failed artist. His character feels like a spiritual successor to Rex from Flight of the Conchords (2007). Sam Rockwell brings manic energy to his dual role as both versions of the story's hero, but the film struggles to balance its multiple tones and narrative layers. Director Jared Hess attempts to recreate the offbeat magic of his breakthrough hit Napoleon Dynamite (2004), but the results feel forced and occasionally mean-spirited.
The film attempts to explore the creative process and the relationship between art and theft, similar to themes found in Big Eyes (2014) and Art School Confidential (2006). It touches on the outsider artist experience and the commodification of creativity, but never fully develops these ideas beyond surface-level quirk.
Coming in the wake of indie comedy successes like Little Miss Sunshine (2006) and Eagle vs Shark (2007), Gentlemen Broncos represents the eventual exhaustion of the mid-2000s quirky indie comedy wave. Its attempts to blend fantasy sequences with awkward comedy feel disconnected, unlike more successful genre hybrids like Scott Pilgrim vs. the World (2010).
The film's exploration of creative ownership and artistic authenticity remains relevant in today's digital age, where content creation and appropriation are constant topics of debate. However, its treatment of these themes lacks the insight found in later works like The Disaster Artist (2017) or Brigsby Bear (2017).
