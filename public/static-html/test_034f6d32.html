Amanda Pierce (Monica Potter), an art restorer at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, moves in with four models after catching her boyfriend cheating. From her new apartment, she becomes infatuated with Jim Winston (Freddie Prinze Jr.), a charming neighbor she believes she witnesses commit murder. The film attempts to blend romantic comedy with elements of Rear Window (1954), but struggles to balance its tonal shifts between slapstick humor and suspense.
Monica Potter tries to channel Julia Roberts-style charm but lacks the magnetic screen presence needed to elevate the material. Freddie Prinze Jr., fresh off teen hits like She's All That (1999), delivers a performance that feels more suited to high school romance than adult romantic thriller. Director Mark Waters, who would later find his footing with Mean Girls (2004), shows early signs of his ability to handle female ensemble dynamics but can't overcome the script's limitations.
Released in 2001, the film represents the tail end of the 90s romantic comedy boom that gave us classics like You've Got Mail (1998) and My Best Friend's Wedding (1997). It attempts to modernize the formula by incorporating thriller elements, following in the footsteps of Miss Congeniality (2000), but the execution feels forced and dated.
The film's attempt to merge romantic comedy with Hitchcockian suspense recalls more successful genre hybrids like Charade (1963) and Manhattan Murder Mystery (1993). However, where those films maintained tonal consistency, Head Over Heels lurches awkwardly between broad comedy and attempted thriller sequences.
The film's portrayal of the modeling industry and gender dynamics feels particularly dated when compared to more nuanced modern examinations like The Devil Wears Prada (2006). Its attempted commentary on appearances versus reality might have worked better in a straightforward romantic comedy format.
