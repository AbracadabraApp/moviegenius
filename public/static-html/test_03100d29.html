Pusher II follows Tonny, a small-time criminal recently released from prison, as he attempts to navigate his complicated relationship with his ruthless gangster father while trying to prove himself in Copenhagen's criminal underworld. When he discovers he has a newborn son, Tonny must confront his own identity as both a son and father. The film's raw emotional core centers on Tonny's desperate attempts to earn his father's respect while simultaneously questioning whether he's destined to repeat the same toxic patterns with his own child.
Mads Mikkelsen's portrayal of Tonny is a masterclass in vulnerability beneath a tough exterior, predating his more famous roles in Casino Royale (2006) and Hannibal (2013-2015). Refn's direction builds on the stark realism of the original Pusher (1996), but with a more refined visual style that would later influence his work in Drive (2011). The handheld camera work and natural lighting create an immediate, documentary-like atmosphere that pulls viewers into Tonny's world.
The film's exploration of toxic masculinity and generational trauma within criminal families preceded similar themes in works like Animal Kingdom (2010) and The Place Beyond the Pines (2012). It offers a brutal critique of patriarchal power structures while examining how violence and emotional abuse cycle through generations. The Copenhagen setting provides a unique glimpse into the universal nature of these dynamics, showing how crime and family dysfunction transcend cultural boundaries.
Within the crime genre, Pusher II stands out for its focus on emotional vulnerability rather than glorifying violence. It shares DNA with character-driven crime films like Mean Streets (1973) and A Prophet (2009), where the criminal element serves as a backdrop for deeper personal struggles. The film helped establish the new wave of Nordic noir that would later influence international crime cinema.
The film's examination of masculine identity, fatherhood, and the struggle to break destructive patterns remains powerfully relevant. Its influence can be seen in recent works like The Kindergarten Teacher (2018) and You Were Never Really Here (2017), which similarly explore damaged individuals trying to find redemption in a violent world.
