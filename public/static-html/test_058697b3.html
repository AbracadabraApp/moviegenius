This unflinching documentary traces the complex evolution of the skinhead movement from its origins in 1960s British working-class culture through its various modern incarnations. Director Daniel Schweizer gains remarkable access to both anti-racist SHARP (Skinheads Against Racial Prejudice) members and neo-Nazi groups, letting them tell their stories in their own words. The film moves between Europe and North America, revealing how a youth subculture rooted in Jamaican music and multiracial unity became partially co-opted by white supremacist ideologies.
Schweizer's directorial approach emphasizes raw authenticity over stylistic flourishes, allowing his subjects to express themselves freely while maintaining critical distance. The film's structure cleverly juxtaposes opposing viewpoints, highlighting the stark ideological divide within the broader skinhead culture. The use of archival footage effectively traces the movement's evolution, while contemporary interviews capture the passionate intensity of current participants.
Like American History X (1998) and This Is England (2006), the film examines how economic uncertainty and social alienation can drive youth toward extremist ideologies. It's particularly relevant in our current era of rising political polarization and resurgent far-right movements. The documentary's exploration of how music and fashion choices become powerful markers of identity recalls similar themes in The Decline of Western Civilization (1981).
Within the tradition of subcultural documentaries like The Devil and Daniel Johnston (2005) and Gimme Shelter (1970), Skinhead Attitude stands out for its balanced examination of a controversial subject. It follows the observational approach pioneered by films like Don't Look Back (1967) while incorporating the social analysis of modern documentaries like The Act of Killing (2012).
The film's examination of how youth movements can be co-opted by extremist ideologies remains deeply relevant to contemporary discussions about online radicalization and political violence. Its insights into the intersection of music, fashion, and politics echo through more recent documentaries like White Noise (2020) and Alt-Right: Age of Rage (2018).
