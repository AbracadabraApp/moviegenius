Set during the Boxer Rebellion in 1900 China, '55 Days at Peking' follows the siege of the foreign legations' compounds in Beijing. Major Matt Lewis (Charlton Heston) represents the American forces, while Sir Arthur Robertson (David Niven) leads the British contingent as they defend against Chinese nationalist forces. The film captures the complex political dynamics between Western powers and Chinese sovereignty, centered around the 55-day siege that tested diplomatic relations and military might. The personal drama unfolds through Lewis's relationship with Russian Baroness Natalie Ivanoff (Ava Gardner), adding human dimension to the geopolitical crisis.
Nicholas Ray brings his characteristic attention to human relationships amid grand historical events, similar to his approach in Rebel Without a Cause (1955). Heston delivers the kind of commanding performance that defined his historical epics like The Ten Commandments (1956), while David Niven brings diplomatic gravitas to his role. The film's massive sets and thousands of extras create a sense of scale reminiscent of Ben-Hur (1959), though with a more intimate focus on the diplomatic compound.
The film reflects the complex legacy of Western imperialism in China, though viewed through a 1960s lens. While it attempts to present Chinese grievances, its perspective remains decidedly Western, similar to contemporary epics like Lawrence of Arabia (1962). Modern viewers may find its portrayal of Chinese characters problematic, but the film's examination of cultural conflict and nationalism remains relevant.
As part of the historical epic boom of the 1960s, '55 Days at Peking' stands alongside films like El Cid (1961) and The Fall of the Roman Empire (1964) in its ambitious scope and production values. It represents the tail end of Hollywood's golden age of epics, before changing tastes and budgetary constraints led to their decline.
The film's themes of international cooperation, cultural misunderstanding, and nationalism echo in modern geopolitics. Its depiction of Western powers navigating Chinese politics parallels contemporary discussions about international relations with China, though modern films like The Last Emperor (1987) offer more nuanced perspectives on Chinese history.
