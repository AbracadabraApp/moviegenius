Virgil Starkwell's journey from a troubled youth to an incompetent bank robber unfolds through a revolutionary mockumentary format that would influence comedy for decades. Allen's hapless criminal can't seem to get anything right - from his hilariously botched robberies to his prison escape attempts with a gun made of soap. The film's genius lies in treating this bumbling criminal's story with the gravitas of a serious crime documentary, complete with interviews, archival footage, and deadpan narration.
Allen's performance as Virgil establishes his iconic neurotic persona that would define his career. His physical comedy and timing are impeccable, especially in scenes like the infamous marching band sequence. The documentary style allows for creative storytelling devices, with interview subjects directly contradicting each other and 'archival footage' that becomes increasingly absurd. This was revolutionary for 1969, predating This Is Spinal Tap (1984) by fifteen years.
The film cleverly satirizes both true-crime documentaries and the romantic gangster films of the 1930s like Public Enemy (1931). It captures the anti-establishment mood of the late 1960s while poking fun at society's fascination with criminals. The film's influence can be seen in modern mockumentaries like What We Do in the Shadows (2014) and shows like The Office (2005-2013).
As one of the earliest mockumentaries, it established many conventions of the genre. Its influence extends through Zelig (1983), This Is Spinal Tap (1984), and even modern works like Borat (2006). The film bridges the gap between slapstick comedy and sophisticated satire, paving the way for intelligent comedy that doesn't sacrifice laughs for smarts.
The film's commentary on media representation and celebrity criminals remains relevant today. Its innovative mixing of formats and meta-commentary on documentary filmmaking anticipated our current era of fake news and documentary manipulation. The humor still lands, proving that well-crafted comedy transcends its era.
