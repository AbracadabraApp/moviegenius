Les Cousins follows two cousins in Paris - the provincial, serious Charles and the decadent, charismatic Paul. When Charles arrives to study law, he's drawn into Paul's hedonistic world of parties and moral ambiguity. Their relationship becomes further complicated when both fall for Florence, creating a tense triangle that explores innocence versus corruption. The film brilliantly subverts expectations by having the seemingly virtuous Charles become increasingly unstable while the apparently amoral Paul reveals hidden depths.
Chabrol directs with remarkable assurance in only his second feature, crafting a morally complex tale that eschews easy judgments. Gérard Blain and Jean-Claude Brialy deliver nuanced performances that capture the cousins' opposing worldviews while suggesting their underlying similarities. The film's visual style, with Henri Decaë's fluid camerawork, creates an atmosphere of mounting tension and moral ambiguity that would influence countless New Wave films to follow.
The film captures the generational divide in late-1950s France, contrasting traditional provincial values with modern urban sophistication. It critiques both the moral rigidity of the old order and the potential emptiness of modern hedonism. Chabrol's examination of class, privilege, and moral corruption would become recurring themes in French cinema, influencing works like The Conformist (1970) and La Cérémonie (1995).
As an early French New Wave film, Les Cousins helped establish many of the movement's hallmarks: moral ambiguity, complex character psychology, and a break from traditional narrative structures. It shares DNA with other foundational New Wave works like The 400 Blows (1959) and Breathless (1960), while anticipating later psychological dramas like Purple Noon (1960).
The film's exploration of moral relativism, privilege, and the corruption of innocence remains startlingly relevant. Its influence can be seen in modern examinations of youth and morality like The Social Network (2010) and The Great Beauty (2013). The psychological complexity and moral ambiguity continue to resonate with contemporary audiences grappling with similar themes.
