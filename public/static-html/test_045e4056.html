Stage Struck follows the familiar Depression-era backstage musical formula, featuring an aspiring performer (Joan Blondell) trying to make it big on Broadway while navigating romantic complications with a songwriter (Dick Powell). The plot treads well-worn territory as our heroine deals with temperamental stars, demanding producers, and the usual showbiz obstacles, though with less spark than similar films of the era.
While Blondell and Powell demonstrate their reliable chemistry, having previously paired in Gold Diggers of 1935 (1935), their performances here feel somewhat constrained by the routine material. Berkeley's direction shows flashes of his signature style in the production numbers, but lacks the groundbreaking choreography and camera work that defined his earlier masterpieces like Footlight Parade (1933).
The film reflects Depression-era escapism through its backstage musical format, though with less of the social commentary that elevated earlier Warner Bros. musicals. Like Broadway Melody of 1936 (1935), it showcases the period's fascination with show business stories as a vehicle for both entertainment and subtle criticism of the American Dream narrative.
Coming at the tail end of the first great wave of Hollywood musicals, Stage Struck demonstrates how the genre was beginning to show signs of creative exhaustion. While earlier Berkeley films revolutionized the musical with their elaborate production numbers and innovative cinematography, this entry feels more like a routine exercise in familiar tropes.
Modern viewers may find interest in how the film represents the evolution of the backstage musical genre, though contemporary works like La La Land (2016) and The Greatest Showman (2017) handle similar themes with more sophistication and technical prowess. The film primarily serves as a historical document of how Hollywood processed Depression-era anxieties through entertainment.
