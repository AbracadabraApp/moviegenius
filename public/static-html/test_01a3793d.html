In this heartwarming remake of Capra's own Lady for a Day (1933), Apple Annie, a street vendor selling apples and good luck charms in Depression-era New York, lives a double life. Through letters to her daughter in Spain, she's created an elaborate fiction about being a high-society lady. When her daughter announces she's coming to visit with her aristocratic fiancé, local gangster Dave the Dude, who believes Annie's apples bring him luck, orchestrates an elaborate deception to transform the raggedy vendor into a convincing socialite.
Bette Davis shines in a role that demands both comedy and pathos, masterfully portraying Annie's transformation while maintaining the character's essential humanity. Glenn Ford brings charm and complexity to Dave the Dude, while Peter Falk earned an Oscar nomination for his scene-stealing turn as Joy Boy. Capra's direction maintains his signature optimistic touch while addressing themes of class mobility and social pretense.
The film serves as a bridge between Hollywood's Golden Age and the emerging social consciousness of the 1960s. Like My Fair Lady (1964) and Breakfast at Tiffany's (1961), it explores class transformation, but with Capra's distinctive focus on community and collective goodwill. The Depression-era setting resonated with audiences familiar with Capra's earlier works like It's a Wonderful Life (1946) and Mr. Smith Goes to Washington (1939).
As Capra's final film, it represents both a culmination and evolution of his signature style. While maintaining the heartwarming elements of his earlier works, it incorporates darker elements of contemporary gangster films like Some Like It Hot (1959), creating a unique blend of comedy, drama, and social commentary that influenced later films like Trading Places (1983).
The film's themes of identity, social mobility, and the power of community support remain startlingly relevant. Its exploration of how people present themselves to the world anticipates modern discussions about social media personas and authentic identity. The story's emphasis on collective kindness and social responsibility echoes in contemporary films like The Grand Budapest Hotel (2014) and Little Women (2019).
