Blonde presents a fictionalized and deeply controversial portrayal of Marilyn Monroe's life, from her troubled childhood as Norma Jeane to her rise to stardom and eventual decline. Ana de Armas delivers a committed performance as Monroe, but the film's relentless focus on trauma and victimization creates a disturbing spectacle that often feels exploitative rather than illuminating. The narrative weaves through Monroe's relationships, including her marriages to Joe DiMaggio and Arthur Miller, while consistently returning to themes of abuse, abandonment, and Hollywood's destructive nature.
De Armas's transformation is remarkable, capturing Monroe's vulnerability and screen presence with uncanny precision. However, Dominik's direction seems more interested in creating artistic tableaux of suffering than exploring Monroe's complexity as an artist and human being. The film's stunning black-and-white photography alternates with color sequences, reminiscent of Raging Bull (1980), but the technical virtuosity feels hollow against the film's exploitative undertones.
While attempting to critique Hollywood's objectification of women, Blonde paradoxically perpetuates the very exploitation it claims to condemn. Unlike My Week with Marilyn (2011), which sought to humanize its subject, this film reduces Monroe to a series of traumas. The approach raises serious questions about biographical responsibility and the ethics of posthumous portrayal, especially when compared to more nuanced biopics like Jackie (2016).
Within the biographical drama genre, Blonde stands as an experimental outlier, more aligned with surrealist psychological horror than traditional biopics. While films like Spencer (2021) and I'm Not There (2007) successfully reimagined their subjects through artistic abstraction, Blonde's innovations feel more gratuitous than revelatory. The film's NC-17 rating speaks to its graphic approach, marking a controversial departure from conventional biographical storytelling.
In the era of #MeToo and renewed discussions about Hollywood's treatment of women, Blonde's release feels particularly tone-deaf. While attempting to expose industry exploitation, it creates its own problematic spectacle. The film's approach contrasts sharply with modern biopics like Judy (2019), which manage to address similar themes of fame and exploitation while maintaining their subject's dignity.
