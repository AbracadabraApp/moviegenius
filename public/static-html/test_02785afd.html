Passenger follows Liza, a former SS guard at Auschwitz, who encounters a former prisoner while on a luxury cruise with her husband. Through fragmentary flashbacks and conflicting narratives, we see how Liza remembers her relationship with prisoner Marta, either as a compassionate overseer or, more truthfully, as a cruel manipulator. The film's unfinished nature, due to director Munk's death during production, creates a haunting meditation on memory and guilt.
Aleksandra Slaska delivers a complex performance as Liza, portraying both her present-day persona and flashback sequences with subtle shifts in demeanor. The film's unique structure, combining completed dramatic scenes with still photographs and documentary footage, creates a powerful commentary on the unreliability of memory and historical truth. Munk's vision, completed by his colleagues after his death, pioneered a new form of Holocaust narrative.
Released less than two decades after World War II, Passenger represented a crucial shift in Holocaust cinema, moving beyond simple victim-perpetrator dynamics to examine the complex psychology of guilt and self-deception. The film's innovative approach influenced later works like Shoah (1985) and The Reader (2008), establishing new ways to address Holocaust memory.
Within Polish cinema of the 1960s, Passenger stands alongside works like Ashes and Diamonds (1958) in examining war's moral complexity. Its experimental structure predates similar techniques used in Hiroshima Mon Amour (1959) and Night and Fog (1956), blending documentary and fiction to confront historical trauma.
The film's examination of historical memory and self-justification remains startlingly relevant, particularly in an era of rising historical revisionism. Its techniques influence contemporary works like Son of Saul (2015) and The White Ribbon (2009), showing how past atrocities continue to shape present consciousness.
