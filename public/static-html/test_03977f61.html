In this satirical dive into internet fame, Maya Hawke plays Frankie, a struggling filmmaker who discovers Jake (Andrew Garfield), a charismatic yet volatile personality who becomes a viral sensation. Their partnership spawns a YouTube channel that rockets them to digital stardom, but success quickly reveals the toxic underbelly of influencer culture. Garfield's performance as Jake is deliberately overwhelming - a manic, twisted reflection of social media excess that oscillates between fascinating and grating.
Gia Coppola's direction aims for a Network-meets-Black Mirror vibe, with Garfield swinging for the fences in an unhinged performance that channels both Howard Beale's famous rants and modern YouTube personalities. Hawke brings a grounded presence as Frankie, though her character's arc feels underdeveloped. The film's visual style employs aggressive digital aesthetics - screen graphics, notifications, and viral video formats - that sometimes overshadow the human elements.
While attempting to critique internet culture and viral fame, Mainstream often falls into the same superficiality it condemns. Its commentary on digital narcissism and online authenticity treads familiar ground covered more effectively in films like Eighth Grade (2018) and Searching (2018). The movie's attempts to shock and provoke through Garfield's increasingly outrageous behavior feel more desperate than insightful.
As a social media satire, Mainstream arrives in a crowded landscape of digital age commentaries. Where The Social Network (2010) offered nuanced examination of power and connection, and Ingrid Goes West (2017) provided sharp character study, this film's broader swings at influencer culture lack the same precision. It follows in the tradition of media satires like Network (1976) but struggles to find fresh angles on modern digital life.
Despite its timely subject matter, the film's messaging about social media's corrupting influence feels oddly dated. While platforms like YouTube and Instagram continue evolving, Mainstream's observations remain surface-level, missing opportunities for deeper exploration of how digital fame shapes identity and relationships in the 2020s.
