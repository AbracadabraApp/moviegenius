Set in 1942 Paris, M. Klein follows Robert Klein, a Catholic art dealer profiting from Jewish citizens forced to sell their collections under Nazi occupation. His comfortable life unravels when he discovers another Robert Klein, a Jewish man, whose identity becomes mysteriously entangled with his own. The film's genius lies in its slow-burning psychological tension, as Klein becomes obsessed with finding his namesake while simultaneously trying to prove his own non-Jewish identity to increasingly suspicious authorities.
Alain Delon delivers perhaps his career-best performance, stripping away his trademark cool persona to reveal a man unraveling under existential pressure. Joseph Losey's direction is precise and unnerving, using long takes and careful composition to create a suffocating atmosphere of paranoia. The camera often follows Klein through labyrinthine Parisian streets, reflecting his increasingly confused state of mind and moral position.
The film serves as a devastating critique of French collaboration during WWII, arriving at a time when France was only beginning to reckon with its Vichy past. Like The Conformist (1970) and Army of Shadows (1969), it explores the moral compromises and personal culpability of those who stood by or profited during fascist occupation. The film's examination of identity papers and bureaucratic persecution resonates strongly with contemporary discussions about surveillance and state power.
While it shares elements with political thrillers like Z (1969) and The Day of the Jackal (1973), M. Klein transcends genre conventions to create something more philosophically complex. Its influence can be seen in later films like The Lives of Others (2006) and Phoenix (2014), particularly in their exploration of identity and surveillance in totalitarian contexts.
The film's themes of identity, complicity, and moral responsibility remain startlingly relevant. Its examination of how ordinary people can become entangled in systemic evil speaks to contemporary discussions about social justice and individual responsibility. The bureaucratic nightmare Klein faces eerily prefigures modern concerns about digital identity and state surveillance.
