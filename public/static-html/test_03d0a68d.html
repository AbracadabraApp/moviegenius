Neuf mois follows Samuel, a child psychologist who ironically dreads the idea of having his own children, and his girlfriend Mathilde who unexpectedly becomes pregnant. The film chronicles their tumultuous nine-month journey as Samuel struggles to accept impending fatherhood while Mathilde navigates her own transformative experience. Their story unfolds with characteristic French wit and charm, offering a more nuanced take on pregnancy anxiety than typical Hollywood fare.
Patrick Braoudé pulls double duty as director and lead actor, bringing an authentic vulnerability to Samuel's resistance to change. Philippine Leroy-Beaulieu shines as Mathilde, capturing both the joy and frustration of pregnancy while dealing with an emotionally immature partner. The supporting cast, particularly Catherine Jacob as their friend Sophie, provides perfect comedic timing and emotional grounding. Braoudé's direction maintains an intimate focus on the central relationship while allowing space for broader comedy.
The film captures mid-1990s attitudes toward parenthood and gender roles in France, offering commentary on changing family dynamics and men's evolving role in pregnancy and childrearing. While some elements feel dated, the core emotional struggles remain remarkably relevant. The film's success led to the American remake Nine Months (1995) with Hugh Grant, though the original maintains a more sophisticated approach to the material.
Within the pregnancy comedy genre, Neuf mois stands out for its distinctly French perspective, avoiding the slapstick extremes of films like Junior (1994) while maintaining more emotional depth than Father of the Bride Part II (1995). It shares thematic DNA with later films like Knocked Up (2007) and Away We Go (2009), though with a more grounded European sensibility.
Modern audiences will find the film's exploration of pregnancy anxiety and relationship dynamics surprisingly fresh. While social attitudes have evolved, the core fears about parenthood and adult responsibility remain universal. The film's influence can be seen in contemporary pregnancy narratives like Catastrophe (2015) and Private Life (2018).
