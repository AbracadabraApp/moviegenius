This remake of the 1955 Humphrey Bogart classic follows two escaped convicts who pose as priests in a small border town while trying to flee to Canada. Ned (Robert De Niro) and Jim (Sean Penn) find themselves caught between maintaining their deceptive identities and genuinely connecting with the community they're infiltrating. The premise promises rich comedic potential, but the execution often feels caught between slapstick and serious drama.
De Niro and Penn, both known for intense dramatic roles, attempt to showcase their comedic chops but seem somewhat restrained by the material. Neil Jordan's direction, which had shown such promise in The Company of Wolves (1984) and would later shine in The Crying Game (1992), feels uncertain here. David Mamet's screenplay occasionally sparkles with his trademark dialogue but lacks the sharp edge of his best work like Glengarry Glen Ross (1992).
The film attempts to explore themes of redemption and identity, touching on how roles we play can transform us. This concept would be better executed in later films like Primal Fear (1996) and Catch Me If You Can (2002). The late 1980s setting captures a transitional period in American cinema when comedy-dramas were evolving beyond the broader styles of the early 80s.
Within the prison escape genre, the film sits awkwardly between pure comedy like Stir Crazy (1980) and more serious fare like The Shawshank Redemption (1994). Its attempt to blend religious satire with crime comedy recalls Nuns on the Run (1990) but lacks that film's commitment to its premise.
While themes of identity and redemption remain relevant, the film's treatment feels dated compared to more nuanced modern explorations. Contemporary viewers might find the pacing slow and the comedy-drama balance unsatisfying compared to more focused genre pieces.
