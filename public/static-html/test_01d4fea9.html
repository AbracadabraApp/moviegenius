The Master follows Freddie Quell (Phoenix), a troubled WWII veteran who struggles to readjust to civilian life. His volatile nature and alcoholism lead him to drift until he encounters Lancaster Dodd (Hoffman), the charismatic leader of a philosophical movement called 'The Cause.' Their relationship forms the heart of the film - a complex dance between master and disciple, father and son, manipulator and willing subject. Like Citizen Kane (1941) in its narrative ambition, the film weaves through time while maintaining its focus on the psychological interplay between these two magnetic characters.
Phoenix and Hoffman deliver career-defining performances that recall the intensity of One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (1975). Phoenix embodies Freddie with animal-like rawness, while Hoffman brings both charm and menace to Dodd. Anderson's direction shows the same masterful control he brought to There Will Be Blood (2007), but here he's even more focused on the intimate psychological warfare between his leads. The 70mm photography gives the film a dreamy, period-perfect quality that enhances its exploration of post-war American uncertainty.
While clearly inspired by the early days of Scientology, The Master transcends simple religious critique to examine broader themes of faith, control, and the human need for belonging. Like Network (1976), it's a film about the power of persuasion and the American tendency toward charismatic movements. The post-WWII setting captures a crucial moment when Americans sought new answers and meaning, similar to how Revolutionary Road (2008) explored the cracks in post-war optimism.
The Master represents a high point in the psychological drama genre, following in the tradition of films like Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (1966) and Midnight Cowboy (1969). Anderson's approach to period filmmaking avoids nostalgia in favor of psychological realism, creating a more nuanced view of the era than many contemporary films attempting similar historical explorations.
The film's examination of cult-like devotion and charismatic leadership remains startlingly relevant in our era of social media influence and political tribalism. Its exploration of trauma and the search for meaning echoes through recent films like First Reformed (2017) and Phantom Thread (2017), showing how personal damage can lead to extreme beliefs and dependencies.
