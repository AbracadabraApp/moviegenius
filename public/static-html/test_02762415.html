When their mother leaves for a summer vacation in Australia, the Crandell children think they're in for an easy time, only to face a crisis when their elderly babysitter dies of natural causes. Rather than ruin their mother's trip, 17-year-old Sue Ellen (Christina Applegate) takes charge, faking her way into a high-powered fashion industry job to support her siblings. The film cleverly balances teenage wish fulfillment with genuine stakes, as Sue Ellen navigates adult responsibilities while maintaining the charade of normalcy at home.
Christina Applegate transforms her 'Kelly Bundy' persona from Married... with Children (1987) into something more nuanced and capable. Director Stephen Herek, fresh from Bill & Ted's Excellent Adventure (1989), brings a similar energy to this story of teenagers facing absurd circumstances. The supporting cast, particularly Joanna Cassidy as Sue Ellen's boss Rose, adds credibility to the workplace scenes, grounding the fantasy in relatable office politics.
The film arrived during a wave of home-alone narratives like Home Alone (1990) and Mrs. Doubtfire (1993), but uniquely focuses on teenage responsibility and female empowerment. Sue Ellen's journey from slacker to successful career woman reflects changing attitudes about women in the workplace, while maintaining the fun spirit of films like Working Girl (1988). It's also a time capsule of early 90s fashion, music, and corporate culture.
While following the teen comedy template established by Fast Times at Ridgemont High (1982), the film adds elements of workplace comedy and family drama. It shares DNA with other coming-of-age stories like Risky Business (1983) but with a distinctly feminine perspective. The blend of domestic responsibility and career ambition predates similar themes in The Devil Wears Prada (2006).
Modern audiences will recognize themes of imposter syndrome, work-life balance, and the pressure to grow up quickly. The film's message about finding confidence through adversity remains relevant, while its portrayal of pre-internet problem-solving offers a nostalgic glimpse at analog resourcefulness. Sue Ellen's journey parallels contemporary discussions about young people entering the workforce and navigating adult responsibilities.
