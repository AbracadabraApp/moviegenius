Following the psychological descent of Ernest (Michael Wyle), a disturbed loner working at an adult video store in Los Angeles, 'God's Lonely Man' treads familiar territory in its exploration of urban alienation and vigilante justice. Ernest becomes obsessed with protecting a young runaway named Christiane (Heather McComb), leading him down a violent path of self-proclaimed righteousness against the city's criminal elements.
Michael Wyle's performance, while earnest in its attempt to capture the psychological complexity of his character, often feels like a pale imitation of Robert De Niro's iconic Travis Bickle. The film's direction by Francis von Zerneck shows occasional flourishes of style but ultimately lacks the raw intensity and visual poetry that made similar character studies like Taxi Driver (1976) and The King of Comedy (1982) so compelling.
Released in the mid-1990s, the film attempts to address themes of urban decay, moral corruption, and social isolation that were prevalent in independent cinema of the era. While these themes resonated strongly in films like Falling Down (1993) and Se7en (1995), 'God's Lonely Man' fails to find its own unique voice in the conversation about societal breakdown and vigilante justice.
The film sits within the tradition of psychological character studies about disturbed loners, following the template established by Taxi Driver (1976) and later explored in films like Clean, Shaven (1993). However, it lacks the psychological depth of its predecessors and the technical innovation of later entries in the genre like American Psycho (2000).
While themes of isolation and urban alienation remain relevant, modern examinations like Nightcrawler (2014) and Joker (2019) have pushed the conversation forward in more compelling ways, making 'God's Lonely Man' feel dated and redundant in retrospect.
