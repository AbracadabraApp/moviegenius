Pride and Glory follows the Tierney family, deeply embedded in the NYPD, as they confront corruption within their ranks following the death of four officers. Ray Tierney (Norton) investigates the killings, uncovering a web of corruption that implicates his brother-in-law Jimmy (Farrell). The film explores family loyalty versus professional duty, with Jon Voight's patriarch Francis Tierney Sr. trying to hold his family together while maintaining the department's reputation.
Edward Norton delivers a characteristically intense performance as the morally conflicted Ray, while Colin Farrell brings dangerous unpredictability to Jimmy. Director Gavin O'Connor aims for gritty authenticity in his portrayal of police corruption, though the execution often feels like it's following a well-worn playbook. The winter setting of New York adds atmospheric tension, but can't elevate the familiar narrative beyond standard police drama territory.
The film attempts to examine the complex relationship between family loyalty and professional ethics within law enforcement, a theme that remains relevant amid ongoing discussions about police accountability. However, its treatment of these issues lacks the nuance found in superior entries in the genre like Serpico (1973) or Internal Affairs (1990).
Released in the wake of more innovative police dramas like The Departed (2006) and We Own the Night (2007), Pride and Glory struggles to distinguish itself in an overcrowded genre. While it hits the expected beats of a police corruption thriller, it doesn't bring anything substantially new to the conversation established by classics like Prince of the City (1981).
Though the themes of institutional corruption and family loyalty remain timely, the film's approach feels dated compared to more nuanced modern examinations like End of Watch (2012) or Black and Blue (2019). Its straightforward good-cop-bad-cop narrative doesn't adequately address the complexities of modern policing debates.
