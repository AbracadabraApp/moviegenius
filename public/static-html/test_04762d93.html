Crossfire follows a former CIA operative turned security expert who becomes entangled in a complex web of corruption and violence. The film attempts to capitalize on the post-9/11 action thriller wave but falls short with its formulaic approach. Steven Seagal plays Robert Sikes, delivering his trademark stoic performance in a role that feels recycled from his better works like Under Siege (1992).
The performances are largely underwhelming, with Seagal continuing his late-career pattern of minimal emotional range and limited physical action. The direction lacks the kinetic energy found in contemporary action films like Taken (2008) or the strategic tension of The International (2009). Action sequences feel staged and lack the immediacy that modern audiences expect.
Released during a period when direct-to-video action films were struggling to remain relevant against theatrical blockbusters, Crossfire represents the declining era of 90s-style action heroes. While films like Casino Royale (2006) were reinventing the action genre, Crossfire clings to outdated tropes and storytelling methods.
The film sits awkwardly between the gritty realism of post-Bourne action thrillers and the straightforward approach of 90s action films. Unlike successful hybrid attempts like Safe (2012) or The Equalizer (2014), Crossfire fails to bridge this stylistic gap effectively.
Crossfire serves primarily as a historical marker of the direct-to-video action market's evolution. Its shortcomings highlight why the genre needed the reinvention seen in films like John Wick (2014) and Nobody (2021), which brought fresh energy and innovation to action storytelling.
