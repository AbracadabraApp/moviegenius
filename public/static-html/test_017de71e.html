Set in the neon-soaked world of 1980s fitness culture, 'Killer Workout' follows a series of brutal murders at Rhonda's Workout studio. The film attempts to capitalize on both the aerobics craze popularized by Perfect (1985) and the slasher boom initiated by Friday the 13th (1980). Our protagonist Rhonda runs the gym where someone is killing off spandex-clad victims using an oversized safety pin. The plot throws in red herrings about a disfigured model and suspicious new employees, but ultimately stumbles through its mystery with the grace of a first-time aerobics student.
The performances range from wooden to wildly over-the-top, with Marcia Karr's Rhonda being the most competent among them. Director David A. Prior, known for low-budget action films, tries to merge exploitation elements with genuine horror, resulting in a tonally confused piece that never quite finds its rhythm. The film's attempts at serious dramatic moments are undermined by unintentionally comedic dialogue and bizarre editing choices.
The film unintentionally serves as a time capsule of 1980s fitness culture obsession, when aerobics studios were as ubiquitous as today's CrossFit boxes. Like Perfect (1985) and Staying Alive (1983), it captures the era's fixation on physical perfection and the commodification of fitness, though through a decidedly more exploitative lens. The movie reflects anxieties about body image and the dark side of the fitness craze, albeit in an extremely heavy-handed way.
Coming late in the slasher cycle, 'Killer Workout' represents the genre's desperate attempts to find fresh settings for familiar formulas. While Terror Train (1980) and Prom Night (1980) successfully transported slasher tropes to specific locations, this film's gym setting feels more gimmicky than inspired. It prefigured later fitness-horror entries like Death Spa (1989), though without the self-awareness that made later entries more entertaining.
Today, 'Killer Workout' exists primarily as a curiosity for fans of 80s exploitation cinema and bad movie enthusiasts. Its commentary on fitness culture obsession remains relevant in our Instagram-influenced era, though modern films like Ingrid Goes West (2017) handle similar themes with far more sophistication. The film's earnest attempt to combine aerobics and horror now plays as unintentional comedy, making it a potential candidate for ironic viewing parties.
