Set in neutral Portugal during WWII, The Conspirators follows Vincent Van Der Lyn, a Dutch resistance fighter who flees to Lisbon after sabotaging Nazi operations. He becomes entangled with the mysterious Irene Von Mohr and a network of resistance operatives while being pursued by German agents. The film attempts to recreate the atmosphere of wartime intrigue and romance that made Casablanca (1942) such a success, but struggles to emerge from that film's shadow.
Paul Henreid and Hedy Lamarr generate some chemistry, though their performances feel constrained by the derivative script. Sydney Greenstreet brings his characteristic menacing charm, reminiscent of his work in The Maltese Falcon (1941). Director Jean Negulesco, in one of his early Warner Bros. assignments, shows glimpses of the style that would later flourish in films like Johnny Belinda (1948).
Released during WWII's final years, the film reflects Hollywood's intense focus on resistance fighters and European intrigue. While other films like Watch on the Rhine (1943) and None Shall Escape (1944) brought fresh perspectives to wartime drama, The Conspirators relies too heavily on established formulas. Its portrayal of the resistance movement, while well-intentioned, lacks the authenticity found in contemporary films like This Land Is Mine (1943).
The film belongs to the wartime romantic thriller subgenre that flourished during WWII. While it features the expected elements - exotic location, conflicted romance, Nazi antagonists - it fails to distinguish itself from stronger entries like Notorious (1946) or Paris Underground (1945). The Lisbon setting provides a change from the usual Casablanca locale but isn't utilized to full dramatic effect.
Modern viewers may find interest in the film's depiction of wartime Portugal, a crucial neutral zone during WWII. However, the story's potential for exploring moral ambiguity and political complexity remains largely untapped, especially compared to later examinations like Army of Shadows (1969) or Black Book (2006).
