Miranda July's directorial debut follows Christine (July), a video artist who drives an Elder Cab while pursuing her artistic dreams, and Richard (John Hawkes), a recently separated shoe salesman struggling to connect with his two sons. Their paths intersect in unexpected ways, creating a tapestry of modern relationships, loneliness, and the search for meaningful connection. The film weaves multiple storylines, including Richard's young sons exploring online chat rooms, and Christine's attempts to get her work shown at a contemporary art museum.
July brings her background in performance art to create a uniquely tender vision that feels both experimental and accessible. John Hawkes delivers a beautifully understated performance as Richard, while July herself embodies Christine with an awkward charm that feels authentically human. The film's approach to dialogue and interaction recalls the naturalistic style of Ghost World (2001), while maintaining its own distinct voice.
Released at the dawn of social media, the film presciently explores how technology mediates human connection. It examines loneliness and desire in the digital age with a gentleness reminiscent of Lost in Translation (2003), while tackling themes of artistic expression and authenticity that echo American Beauty (1999). The film's treatment of childhood sexuality and innocence sparked important discussions about representation and boundaries in independent cinema.
The film helped establish a new wave of American independent cinema that blended art house sensibilities with accessible storytelling, paving the way for works like The Squid and the Whale (2005) and Little Miss Sunshine (2006). Its influence can be seen in later films like Frances Ha (2012) and Eighth Grade (2018), which similarly explore contemporary alienation with both humor and empathy.
In an era of increasing digital mediation, the film's exploration of genuine human connection feels more relevant than ever. Its careful observation of how people attempt to bridge emotional distances resonates strongly with contemporary audiences navigating relationships through screens and social media. The film's artistic ambitions and intimate scale influenced a generation of independent filmmakers working in personal storytelling.
