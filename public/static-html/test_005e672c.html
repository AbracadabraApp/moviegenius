Following the events of the first film, Eric Phillips continues his fight against the cyborg law enforcement system that's gone rogue. The sequel amplifies the action but stumbles with a convoluted plot about government conspiracy and artificial intelligence. While Don Wilson brings his martial arts expertise to the role, the narrative feels like a derivative mashup of better cyberpunk films, lacking the depth that made contemporaries like Total Recall (1990) memorable.
Director Richard Pepin delivers standard direct-to-video fare that epitomizes mid-90s low-budget action. The performances are serviceable but constrained by wooden dialogue and predictable character arcs. The film's attempt to capitalize on the cyberpunk trend established by Blade Runner (1982) falls flat, with budget limitations clearly hampering any ambitious visual ideas.
Released during the mid-90s technological boom, CyberTracker 2 reflects period anxieties about surveillance and artificial intelligence, albeit in a superficial way. Unlike more thoughtful entries like Strange Days (1995), it fails to meaningfully engage with its themes of technology and control, instead focusing on standard action sequences.
The film represents the flood of direct-to-video sci-fi action that followed in the wake of Terminator 2: Judgment Day (1991) and RoboCop (1987). While these B-movie offerings helped sustain the genre, they often sacrificed narrative complexity for routine action, as evidenced here.
While its themes of surveillance and AI control remain relevant, modern viewers will find the execution dated and simplistic compared to sophisticated treatments in films like Ex Machina (2014) or Upgrade (2018). The practical effects and stunts hold some nostalgic charm for genre enthusiasts, but offer little to contemporary audiences.
