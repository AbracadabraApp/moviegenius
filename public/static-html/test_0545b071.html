Fandry follows Jabya, a Dalit teenager in rural Maharashtra who falls in love with an upper-caste girl while struggling with his family's poverty and social status. The title refers to pigs, considered unclean, which his family must catch to survive. Through Jabya's eyes, we experience the crushing weight of systemic discrimination and the universal yearning for dignity and acceptance. His pursuit of a black sparrow, believed to possess magical powers that could change his life, becomes a poignant metaphor for his dreams of transcending social barriers.
Director Nagraj Manjule draws from personal experience to craft a devastatingly authentic portrait of caste discrimination. Somnath Avghade delivers a breakthrough performance as Jabya, capturing both adolescent innocence and growing awareness of social injustice. The naturalistic cinematography and use of non-professional actors create a documentary-like immediacy reminiscent of Bicycle Thieves (1948) and Pather Panchali (1955).
Fandry stands alongside Sairat (2016) and Court (2014) in its unflinching examination of caste in modern India. Like Slumdog Millionaire (2008), it reveals harsh social realities through a coming-of-age story, but without Hollywood glamorization. The film's power lies in showing how discrimination operates through subtle everyday interactions and internalized shame, making it more impactful than didactic social dramas.
Within Indian parallel cinema, Fandry builds on the tradition of Ankur (1974) and Sadgati (1981) in addressing caste through personal narratives. Its influence can be seen in subsequent films like Masaan (2015) and Article 15 (2019). The film's neo-realist approach recalls international works like Los Olvidados (1950) in its portrayal of marginalized youth.
As conversations about systemic discrimination intensify globally, Fandry's examination of inherited prejudice and social hierarchies remains urgently relevant. The film's final scene, where Jabya hurls a stone directly at the camera, implicates viewers in society's collective complicity, challenging audiences to confront their own biases and privileges.
