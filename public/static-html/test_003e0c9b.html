In this third installment of the Tammy series, Sandra Dee returns as the charming country girl Tammy, who takes a job at a big city hospital where she inevitably falls for a handsome young intern, Dr. Mark Cheswick (Peter Fonda). The fish-out-of-water story follows Tammy's attempts to adapt to hospital life while bringing her folksy wisdom and cheerful disposition to the sterile medical environment. Her natural charm and homespun remedies clash with modern medical practices, creating both comedic situations and romantic opportunities.
Sandra Dee, already established as America's sweetheart through films like Gidget (1959), brings her trademark innocence to Tammy, though the role feels increasingly constrained by 1963. Peter Fonda, in an early role before his counterculture breakthrough in Easy Rider (1969), appears somewhat uncomfortable in the conventional romantic lead. Director Harry Keller handles the material with a light touch typical of Universal's romantic comedies of the era, but lacks the sophistication found in contemporary works like That Touch of Mink (1962).
The film represents the tail end of the innocent romantic comedy era, released just before the sexual revolution and changing social attitudes would make such naive portrayals feel outdated. Its depiction of medical professionals and hospital dynamics reflects a sanitized, idealistic view common to pre-Medicare America, contrasting sharply with later medical dramas like **M*A*S*H** (1970). The movie's gender politics, with Tammy's primary goal being marriage to a doctor, exemplifies pre-feminist Hollywood attitudes.
While romantic comedies like Breakfast at Tiffany's (1961) were pushing boundaries with more sophisticated themes, 'Tammy and the Doctor' clings to an older formula established by films like State Fair (1945). It represents the sunset of innocent rural-meets-urban romantic comedies that would soon be replaced by more complex explorations of modern relationships.
Modern viewers may find the film's portrayal of healthcare workers and gender roles problematic. Its simplistic approach to romance and career feels particularly dated in an era of complex medical dramas like Grey's Anatomy. However, it serves as an interesting time capsule of early 1960s social values and Hollywood's idealized version of medical professionals.
