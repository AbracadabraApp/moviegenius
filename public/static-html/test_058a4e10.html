Waru presents eight ten-minute vignettes, each shot in a single take, focusing on different women connected by the death of a young boy named Waru. Each segment occurs at the same time on the morning of Waru's tangi (funeral), offering distinct perspectives on child abuse, cultural identity, and community responsibility. The innovative structure recalls films like Amores Perros (2000) and 21 Grams (2003), but with a uniquely Indigenous perspective that grounds the interconnected narratives in Māori traditions and contemporary realities.
The collaborative direction from eight Māori women filmmakers creates a powerful mosaic of perspectives, each bringing their distinct voice while maintaining thematic cohesion. The single-take format, reminiscent of Birdman (2014), adds immediacy and raw emotional power to each segment. The performances, particularly from the female ensemble cast, carry authentic weight and cultural specificity that elevates the material beyond conventional drama.
Like Once Were Warriors (1994), Waru confronts difficult social issues within Māori communities while celebrating cultural resilience. The film addresses child abuse, domestic violence, and cultural displacement with unflinching honesty, similar to how Precious (2009) tackled systemic issues affecting marginalized communities. The multiple perspectives echo Rashomon (1950), but with a focus on collective responsibility rather than conflicting truths.
Within the context of Indigenous cinema, Waru stands alongside works like Whale Rider (2002) and Boy (2010) in representing authentic Māori experiences. Its innovative structure and female-driven narrative connects to the broader movement of experimental narrative films like Three Times (2005), while its social commentary aligns with contemporary films addressing systemic inequality like Moonlight (2016).
The film's exploration of community responsibility and cultural trauma remains deeply relevant, particularly in discussions about Indigenous rights and representation. Its groundbreaking production model, featuring all-female Māori directors, has influenced subsequent projects like Vai (2019), demonstrating new possibilities for collaborative Indigenous storytelling.
