The Gentle Sex follows seven British women from different backgrounds who join the Auxiliary Territorial Service (ATS) during World War II. Through their interconnected stories, we witness their transformation from civilians to military personnel, challenging the era's gender expectations. The film's strength lies in its authentic portrayal of women's wartime experiences, from basic training to their vital roles supporting the war effort.
Leslie Howard's direction brings a sensitive touch to what could have been mere propaganda. His narration adds a personal dimension, while the ensemble cast delivers naturalistic performances that avoid melodramatic clichés common in wartime films. The documentary-style approach to certain sequences lends authenticity, making it feel more like a historical record than typical war drama.
Released at the height of WWII, the film represents a crucial shift in depicting women's roles in wartime society. Unlike the glamorized portrayals in Mrs. Miniver (1942), The Gentle Sex offers a more grounded view of women's military service. It stands as an important precursor to later films like Their Finest (2016) and Land Girls (1998) that explore women's wartime contributions.
Within the context of British wartime cinema, The Gentle Sex distinguishes itself by focusing exclusively on women's military experience, contrasting with male-centric narratives like In Which We Serve (1942). Its influence can be seen in later works such as Millions Like Us (1943) and modern interpretations like A League of Their Own (1992).
The film's examination of women breaking into traditionally male-dominated spaces remains relevant today. Its themes of female empowerment and solidarity echo in contemporary military dramas like G.I. Jane (1997) and Private Benjamin (1980), while its exploration of gender roles in crisis times connects to modern discussions about workplace equality and military integration.
