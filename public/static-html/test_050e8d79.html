Joe is a traumatized veteran who rescues trafficked girls while battling his own demons. When a job to save a senator's daughter goes wrong, he descends into a nightmare of corruption and violence. The film's brutal efficiency mirrors Joe's damaged psyche, with Ramsay's direction keeping us trapped in his fractured perspective. Unlike conventional revenge thrillers, the violence here is brief, brutal, and often obscured - we feel its impact rather than witness its execution.
Phoenix inhabits Joe with a physical and psychological weight that recalls Robert De Niro in Taxi Driver (1976). His performance is a masterclass in minimalist acting, conveying volumes through subtle gestures and pained silences. Ramsay's direction eschews action movie conventions, instead creating a hypnotic study of trauma and redemption that shares DNA with Drive (2011) and Leon: The Professional (1994).
The film confronts human trafficking and institutional corruption while examining how violence begets violence. It's a scathing critique of power structures that protect the wealthy while exploiting the vulnerable. Like First Reformed (2017), it explores how past trauma shapes present actions, while its themes of rescue and redemption echo Prisoners (2013).
Ramsay reinvents the vigilante thriller genre, stripping away glamorization to expose raw psychological truth. The film's approach to violence and redemption shares thematic DNA with Oldboy (2003) and A History of Violence (2005), while its experimental narrative structure recalls Memento (2000).
In an era of increasing awareness about mental health and trauma, the film's unflinching portrayal of PTSD and childhood abuse remains painfully relevant. Its exploration of systemic corruption and power dynamics echoes contemporary discussions about institutional abuse and accountability.
