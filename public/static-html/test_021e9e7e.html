This intimate documentary follows Nobel Peace Prize winner Malala Yousafzai, the Pakistani schoolgirl who survived a Taliban assassination attempt after speaking out for girls' education. The film weaves together her extraordinary story of survival with tender moments of her current life as both a global activist and a regular teenager. Through personal footage and interviews, we see Malala's close relationship with her father Ziauddin, who named her after a legendary Afghan heroine and encouraged her outspoken advocacy from an early age.
Director Davis Guggenheim, known for An Inconvenient Truth (2006), brings his signature style of combining intimate personal moments with broader social issues. The film's strength lies in capturing Malala's duality - she's both an extraordinary activist and a normal teen who giggles about cricket players and struggles with homework. The animation sequences, reminiscent of Persepolis (2007), beautifully illustrate the historical and cultural context of her story.
The film arrives at a crucial moment in the global conversation about women's rights, education, and religious extremism. Like Girl Rising (2013), it demonstrates the transformative power of education and the courage of those who fight for it. The documentary draws parallels with other youth activists and reminds us of the ongoing struggles for girls' education worldwide, echoing themes from On the Way to School (2013).
Within the landscape of social justice documentaries, this film stands alongside powerful works like I Am Malala (2015) and He Named Me Malala joins the tradition of intimate biographical documentaries like Jane (2017) and RBG (2018), which profile remarkable women who've changed the world. The film's approach to mixing personal narrative with global issues recalls Born into Brothels (2004).
As extremism and attacks on education continue worldwide, Malala's story remains urgently relevant. The film's themes of courage, resilience, and the power of education echo in contemporary movements like March For Our Lives and climate activism. Its exploration of father-daughter relationships and family sacrifice in the pursuit of justice connects with audiences across cultures.
