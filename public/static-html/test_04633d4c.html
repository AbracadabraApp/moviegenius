Nine years after the groundbreaking original, this sequel returns to Basin City's interconnected tales of violence, revenge, and corruption. The film weaves together several storylines, with Eva Green's femme fatale Ava Lord anchoring the titular 'A Dame to Kill For' segment alongside Josh Brolin's Dwight McCarthy. Joseph Gordon-Levitt joins as Johnny, a cocky gambler taking on Powers Boothe's corrupt Senator Roark, while Jessica Alba's Nancy Callahan pursues vengeance for John Hartigan's death. While the pieces are in place for compelling noir storytelling, the narrative feels more scattered and less impactful than its predecessor.
Eva Green delivers the film's standout performance, embodying the manipulative femme fatale archetype with seductive menace reminiscent of Barbara Stanwyck in Double Indemnity (1944). Mickey Rourke's Marv remains a brutal force of nature, though his appearances feel more obligatory this time. Rodriguez and Miller's direction maintains the stark black-and-white aesthetic with splashes of color, but the visual impact feels diminished, lacking the shocking originality that made the first film so revolutionary.
The film's treatment of gender and violence feels more problematic in 2014 than it did in 2005, with its stylized brutality and female characterization appearing dated rather than subversive. While the original effectively channeled the spirit of classic film noir like The Maltese Falcon (1941), this sequel struggles to justify its existence beyond visual spectacle.
Coming after neo-noir evolutions like Drive (2011) and Blade Runner 2049 (2017), 'A Dame to Kill For' feels stuck in the past. The comic book noir approach that felt revolutionary in 2005 now seems like a technical exercise rather than a genuine progression of the genre. The film sits awkwardly between homage and innovation, never quite achieving either.
The film's release coincided with a broader conversation about representation in cinema, making its pulpy exploitation elements feel out of step with contemporary sensibilities. While movies like Gone Girl (2014) were finding new ways to subvert noir tropes, 'A Dame to Kill For' remains anchored to an increasingly dated aesthetic and worldview.
