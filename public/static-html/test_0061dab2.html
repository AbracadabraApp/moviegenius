Lilting follows Richard (Ben Whishaw), who attempts to connect with his deceased partner Kai's Chinese-Cambodian mother Junn (Pei-pei Cheng), despite their inability to speak each other's language. Through an interpreter, they navigate their shared loss while confronting cultural differences and unspoken truths. The film delicately weaves between past and present, memory and reality, as both characters struggle to find common ground in their grief.
Ben Whishaw delivers a quietly devastating performance reminiscent of his work in Bright Star (2009), while Pei-pei Cheng brings tremendous dignity and complexity to Junn. Director Hong Khaou's debut feature demonstrates remarkable restraint, allowing silence and untranslated dialogue to speak volumes. The film's intimate cinematography recalls the close character studies of In the Mood for Love (2000), creating a similarly hypnotic atmosphere of longing and loss.
Like The Farewell (2019), Lilting expertly navigates the complexities of Eastern and Western cultural dynamics while exploring universal themes of family and loss. The film breaks new ground in its intersection of LGBTQ+ themes with traditional Chinese family values, similar to Happy Together (1997) but from a uniquely British-Asian perspective. Its handling of language barriers echoes Lost in Translation (2003), but with higher emotional stakes.
Within the landscape of LGBTQ+ cinema, Lilting stands apart by focusing not on coming out or relationship drama, but on the aftermath of loss and the complex dynamics of family acceptance. It shares DNA with films like Weekend (2011) and Moonlight (2016) in its quiet, naturalistic approach to queer storytelling, while its exploration of grief connects to films like Truly Madly Deeply (1990).
The film's themes of cross-cultural communication and understanding remain vitally relevant, particularly in increasingly multicultural societies. Its sensitive portrayal of grief and the challenges of connecting across cultural and linguistic divides speaks to universal experiences of loss and the human need for connection, similar to more recent works like Minari (2020).
