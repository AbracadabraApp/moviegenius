A luxury ocean liner gets capsized by a rogue wave on New Year's Eve, forcing a small group of survivors to navigate their way up through the inverted ship's infrastructure to reach the bottom (now top) hull. Led by professional gambler Dylan Johns (Josh Lucas) and former firefighter Robert Ramsey (Kurt Russell), the group faces flooding chambers, electrical hazards, and unstable structures while racing against time. Despite its impressive cast, the characters remain thinly sketched, serving mainly as pieces to move through elaborate action sequences.
Director Wolfgang Petersen, known for the submarine classic Das Boot (1981), brings his expertise in claustrophobic maritime tension, but gets overwhelmed by CGI spectacle. The talented ensemble cast, including Richard Dreyfuss and Emmy Rossum, struggles with underwritten roles. The film prioritizes visual effects over the human drama that made the 1972 original so compelling, though the technical achievement of creating a massive upside-down ship environment is impressive.
Released in an era of disaster film revivals like The Day After Tomorrow (2004) and War of the Worlds (2005), Poseidon reflects Hollywood's renewed interest in catastrophe spectacles post-9/11. However, unlike those films which addressed contemporary anxieties, Poseidon feels more like a technical exercise in digital effects than a meaningful exploration of survival and human nature.
The film follows the disaster movie template established by classics like The Towering Inferno (1974) and Airport (1970), but lacks their character-driven approach. While modern disaster films like Titanic (1997) successfully balanced spectacle with emotional investment, Poseidon struggles to find that equilibrium, focusing more on set-pieces than storytelling.
The film's emphasis on visual effects over substance foreshadowed the industry's increasing reliance on CGI spectacle. While technically proficient, it demonstrates why practical effects-driven classics like The Abyss (1989) and character-focused disaster films like Deep Impact (1998) maintain their impact better over time.
