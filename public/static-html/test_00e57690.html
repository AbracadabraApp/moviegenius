Symbiopsychotaxiplasm: Take One defies conventional description, operating simultaneously as a documentary, an experiment, and a meta-commentary on filmmaking itself. Director William Greaves films actors performing a scene in Central Park while also filming the crew filming the scene, creating multiple layers of reality. The core scene involves a relationship dispute, but the real drama unfolds in the tensions between crew members questioning Greaves's methods and intentions.
Greaves's revolutionary vision places himself at the center of a cinematic experiment where he appears to lose control while maintaining precise orchestration of chaos. The crew's genuine frustration and confusion become part of the narrative, blurring lines between performance and reality. The film crew's impromptu meetings about the director's competence create a fascinating power dynamic that questions traditional filmmaking hierarchies.
Released in 1968, the film reflects the era's questioning of authority and traditional structures. It anticipates reality TV and meta-commentary decades before they became mainstream, while addressing racial dynamics in filmmaking through Greaves's presence as an African American director. The film's experimental nature aligns with the period's avant-garde movements while pushing boundaries further than many contemporaries dared.
This film revolutionized documentary form, predating similar meta-approaches in films like Man with a Movie Camera (1929) and F for Fake (1973). Its influence can be seen in later works like Sherman's March (1986) and Exit Through the Gift Shop (2010), where the filmmaker's presence becomes integral to the narrative. The multiple-camera approach and layered reality anticipated modern documentary techniques by decades.
In our era of reality TV, behind-the-scenes content, and meta-storytelling, Symbiopsychotaxiplasm feels remarkably prescient. Its exploration of power dynamics, reality versus performance, and the filmmaking process remains deeply relevant to contemporary discussions about media creation and consumption. The film's questions about authority, truth, and representation continue to resonate in our digital age.
