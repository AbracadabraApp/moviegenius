A direct parody of The Exorcist (1973), Repossessed reunites Linda Blair with the demon possession genre that made her famous, this time playing Nancy Aglet, who becomes possessed again 17 years after her original exorcism. Leslie Nielsen stars as Father Mayii, a bumbling exorcist who must team up with Father Luke Brophy (Ned Beatty) to save Nancy's soul. The film attempts to combine supernatural horror tropes with slapstick comedy, incorporating elements of televangelism satire and pop culture references from the late 1980s.
Nielsen brings his trademark deadpan delivery that served him so well in Airplane! (1980) and the Police Squad series, but the material doesn't match his comedic talents. Linda Blair shows good sport in parodying her iconic role from The Exorcist, but the script gives her little to work with beyond obvious callbacks to the original film. Director Bob Logan attempts to recreate the success of other horror parodies like Young Frankenstein (1974), but lacks the sharp wit and clever writing that made those films classics.
The film arrived at the tail end of the 1980s parody boom, when audiences were becoming oversaturated with spoof films. Its attempted satire of televangelism and religious programming feels tame compared to more biting commentaries like Network (1976) or even Pray TV (1980). The movie represents a particular moment when horror parodies were transitioning from clever genre deconstructions to more obvious joke-delivery systems.
While Scary Movie (2000) would later revitalize the horror parody genre for a new generation, Repossessed stands as an example of diminishing returns in the spoof genre of the late 1980s and early 1990s. It lacks the clever writing of Dead Men Don't Wear Plaid (1982) or the cultural impact of This Is Spinal Tap (1984), instead relying on broad humor and obvious references that don't hold up well over time.
Modern viewers may find interest in the film as a cultural artifact of how parody films evolved, but its dated references and shallow humor make it less appealing than contemporary horror-comedies like What We Do in the Shadows (2014) or Tucker & Dale vs. Evil (2010). The film's greatest value might be as a study in how not to construct a genre parody.
