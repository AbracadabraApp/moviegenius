The Love Witch follows Elaine, a modern-day witch who uses spells and magic to make men fall in love with her, with deadly consequences. After leaving San Francisco following her husband's mysterious death, she moves to a Victorian apartment in Northern California to start fresh. Using a combination of spells, potions, and her own seductive powers, she begins a quest to find her perfect love, leaving a trail of destruction in her wake. Samantha Robinson delivers a mesmerizing performance as Elaine, channeling the glamour of classic Hollywood while adding layers of complexity to what could have been a one-note character.
Writer-director Anna Biller's meticulous attention to detail creates a stunning homage to 1960s Technicolor thrillers like Bell, Book and Candle (1958) and Blood and Lace (1971). Every aspect of the production, from the costumes to the set design, was crafted to achieve authentic period aesthetics while serving the film's modern feminist message. The film was shot on 35mm film, giving it the rich, saturated look of classics like Suspiria (1977) and The Birds (1963).
The Love Witch brilliantly subverts the 'deadly woman' tropes seen in films like Basic Instinct (1992) and Fatal Attraction (1987) by examining female fantasy, desire, and the male gaze through a distinctly feminist lens. Biller's script cleverly deconstructs romantic movie clichés while exploring how patriarchal expectations of love and beauty can drive both men and women to destructive extremes.
The film exists at a fascinating intersection of horror, comedy, and art house cinema, drawing influence from exploitation films like Beyond the Valley of the Dolls (1970) while elevating the material through sophisticated social commentary. It shares DNA with other feminist horror works like Jennifer's Body (2009) and A Girl Walks Home Alone at Night (2014), using genre elements to explore themes of female empowerment and revenge.
In an era of renewed focus on feminist storytelling and discussions about toxic relationships, The Love Witch feels increasingly relevant. Its exploration of gender roles, power dynamics, and the dark side of romantic fantasy resonates with contemporary conversations while paying tribute to the visual language of classic cinema.
