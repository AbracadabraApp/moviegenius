This remarkable documentary constructs its narrative entirely from official state footage and home movies of Romanian dictator Nicolae Ceausescu. Without voice-over or explanatory text, the film presents Ceausescu's carefully curated public image chronologically from his rise to power in 1965 to his execution in 1989. We see him meeting world leaders, addressing massive rallies, and enjoying privileged moments of leisure - all while his regime brutally controlled Romania's population.
Director Andrei Ujica spent four years assembling and editing over 1,000 hours of archival footage. The genius lies in how he allows the propaganda to speak for itself, revealing the absurdity of personality cults through careful juxtaposition. Ceausescu unknowingly performs for us, his carefully managed public appearances taking on sinister new meaning with historical hindsight. The film's approach recalls the observational power of The Emperor's Naked Army Marches On (1987) and the archival mastery of The Sorrow and the Pity (1969).
The film provides devastating insight into how authoritarian regimes construct their image and maintain power through media manipulation. Like The Last Days of the USSR (2021) and The Day of the Sun (2015), it shows how dictatorships use ceremony and spectacle to project strength while masking oppression. The footage of massive synchronized rallies particularly echoes Triumph of the Will (1935), though Ujica's critical framing completely inverts Riefenstahl's celebratory intent.
This film revolutionizes the archival documentary form by proving that raw footage alone, skillfully edited, can be more powerful than explanatory devices. It builds on techniques pioneered in The Fall of the Romanov Dynasty (1927) while anticipating works like State Funeral (2019). The approach has influenced recent films like The Reagan Show (2017) and Our Nixon (1995) in their examination of political image-making.
In our era of carefully managed political personas and media manipulation, this film's examination of how power presents itself remains urgently relevant. It offers crucial lessons about recognizing propaganda and understanding how authoritarian leaders craft their public image. The film's technique of letting historical footage speak for itself provides a powerful model for examining contemporary political figures and movements.
